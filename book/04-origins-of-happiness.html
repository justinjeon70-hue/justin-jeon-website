Part Two: Oxytocin for the Soul

Chapter 4: The Origins of Happiness

Years ago, I came across a book called The Origins of Happiness by Professor Eun-Kook Suh of Yonsei University. I sat down and did not stand up again until the last page fell shut. The next day, still haunted by its afterglow, I opened it and read it a second time. Then I hunted down the references.1

The passage that lodged most deeply in my memory was a finding so counterintuitive it felt almost offensive: the brain regions that register physical pain and social pain are identical. The agony of losing a leg in a sudden accident — of wondering how you will ever earn a living again — fires in the same neural circuitry as the agony of being cut from the group and facing the world alone. When social pain descends — loneliness, betrayal, the sting of a breakup, the severing of a bond you thought would last — the brain does not merely acknowledge it in the abstract. It generates real, bodily pain, as if evolution itself had decided that isolation was a wound worth screaming about.

Here is the evidence. Sixty-two college students were divided into two groups. One received a common painkiller — acetaminophen, the active ingredient in Tylenol. The other received a placebo. The students who took Tylenol reported feeling significantly less distress from social rejection and relational disconnection.2 Read that again: an over-the-counter headache pill dulled the ache of being excluded. The body makes no distinction between a broken bone and a broken heart.

But the book's most electrifying conclusion was this: it is not successful people who are happy — it is happy people who become successful. Drawing on sociobiological evidence, Professor Suh argues, with considerable force, that success does not deliver happiness the way we imagine it does. We tell ourselves the familiar story: I may be struggling now, but just wait — I will succeed, and then I will finally be happy. Korean dramas and blockbuster films borrow this narrative wholesale, serving up cathartic, triumphant endings to punishingly frustrating storylines — the dramatic equivalent of finally biting into something refreshing after choking on a dry sweet potato, as the Korean idiom goes. The trouble is, the story isn't true.

Extroverts Are Happier

When I recently returned to this book through the lens of my oxytocin research, an entirely different passage leapt off the page. It was the finding that the genes most tightly linked to human happiness are those governing extroversion. Extroverts are far happier than introverts — and even introverts, it turns out, report greater happiness when they are with someone rather than alone. Study after study confirms it. A joint American-Korean research project found that the happier a person was, the more of their waking hours they spent in the company of others. The inverse held, too: unhappy people spent far more time alone. The implication is as simple as it is profound — to be happy, spend more time with other people.3

Some readers will object. But I'm happier alone. Fair enough — yet there is a canyon of difference between choosing solitude and having it thrust upon you. Being alone by will and preference is nothing like wanting to see people but being unable to, or avoiding them because of old wounds, insecurity, or depression that makes every social encounter feel like walking on glass. Just as being unmarried by choice is not the same as being unable to find a partner, voluntary solitude and involuntary isolation are entirely different countries.

In a 2021 global survey asking what matters most in life, South Korea was the only country to place "material wealth" at the top. In virtually every other developed nation — the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Italy, Australia, Japan — "family" came first. Korea may be the only place on earth where "Get rich!" functions as a standard greeting and sincere blessing. This single data point illuminates, with uncomfortable clarity, why Korea's suicide rate ranks so high and its birth rate so stubbornly low. If a nation's priorities are a mirror reflecting its people's consciousness and well-being, then Koreans may have been searching for happiness in altogether the wrong place.

Oxytocin Is the Glue of Relationships

If extroverts are happier, could it be that people with higher oxytocin levels are, too?

The research on extroversion and happiness is robust. The more interesting question is the connection between oxytocin and extroversion itself. Oxytocin is a peptide hormone, which means it cannot do its work without a receptor to catch it. When mutations occur in that receptor, oxytocin's effects diminish — no matter how much of it is coursing through the blood. So what happens to people carrying a mutation in the oxytocin receptor gene known as rs53576? Does their empathy suffer? Does their sociability decline?

It does. Studies have confirmed that people with oxytocin receptor mutations show lower emotional stability and diminished general sociability. Their ability to take another person's perspective — to stand, even momentarily, in someone else's shoes — is impaired. This means that if your oxytocin receptors are not functioning properly, you may struggle to understand others or to read the countless nonverbal cues that lubricate human interaction. The result, almost inevitably, is a personality that tilts toward introversion.

Consider a scene familiar to anyone who has been in a relationship. A girlfriend turns away and says, in that unmistakable tone: "Do I really have to spell everything out for you?" Her boyfriend stands there, bewildered, hands dangling uselessly at his sides. Oxytocin may actually explain this impasse. Women produce four to five times more oxytocin than men, and with it comes a far more refined antenna for nonverbal communication. When that antenna detects nothing on the other end, frustration is inevitable.

Oxytocin in our bodies enables us to feel what others feel — to ache when they ache, to light up when they light up. People with elevated oxytocin are more caring, more encouraging, more trusting, and quicker to forgive. And here is the truly remarkable part: when we extend kindness to someone and witness their face brighten with happiness, our own oxytocin climbs higher still. The molecule operates on a virtuous cycle. People with high oxytocin unconsciously lift the oxytocin of everyone around them, like tuning forks that set the whole room humming.

So what about those whose levels run low? Are they condemned to introversion and unhappiness? Not remotely. The ways to coax oxytocin upward are nearly endless.

Oxytocin in Everyday Life: Pull out a notebook, a planner, or even a napkin. Write down ten things that make you happy.
