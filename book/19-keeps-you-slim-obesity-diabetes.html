Part Five: Oxytocin for a Healthier You

Chapter 19: The Hormone That Keeps You Slim

--- Oxytocin, Obesity, and Diabetes

In 1995, when I began my master's program at the University of Alberta in Canada, I could not have imagined that my career would one day orbit around obesity research. The reason I had crossed the Pacific was to study adapted physical activity under Dr. Robert D. Steadward, chairman of the International Paralympic Committee, whom I had met during a student exchange. At the time, I had little idea how vast the field truly was. Adapted physical activity encompassed exercise physiology, medicine, sociology, psychology, administration — even marketing. The deeper I ventured, the less I could fathom its full depth.

I chose exercise physiology, my abiding passion, and threw myself into graduate work while simultaneously designing exercise programs for people with various disabilities. My particular assignment was to apply functional electrical stimulation to the paralyzed muscles of individuals with spinal cord injuries — inducing contraction and relaxation so that muscles long dormant could power a bicycle, drive a walking motion, or pull at rowing ergometers. Then one day, a realization struck me: virtually every patient I worked with carried significant abdominal fat. If their bellies are this distended, isn't their diabetes risk elevated? Alarmed, I searched the literature using "spinal cord injury" and "diabetes" as keywords. The numbers were staggering: individuals with spinal cord injuries faced a diabetes risk three to five times higher than the general population. And yet not a single published study had examined the role of exercise in this population. From that moment, I seized on a hypothesis — that exercise could dramatically lower diabetes risk by reducing insulin resistance — and my career in metabolic research began.[18-1]

From Diabetes to Obesity

I went on to publish research demonstrating that exercise improves insulin resistance and glycemic control in individuals with spinal cord injuries. That finding led to a deeper question: Why are people with spinal cord injuries so vulnerable to diabetes in the first place? The answer, I discovered, lay in a hormone called leptin. When I entered my doctoral program in 1998, leptin had been identified only four years earlier, and the mechanisms by which it regulated body weight remained largely opaque. Driven by curiosity about why spinal cord injury patients were heavier and more diabetes-prone, I conducted intensive research through the lens of leptin — and became the first to demonstrate that leptin regulates basal metabolic rate via the autonomic nervous system, specifically the sympathetic branch, and that both fasting and exercise influence leptin levels. That work led me to Harvard University's Joslin Diabetes Center, where I studied the brain alongside Dr. Terry Maratos-Flier, focusing on the hypothalamus — the brain's command center for feeding behavior.[18-2]

Here is where the story converges with oxytocin. The anterior pituitary, anatomically linked to the hypothalamus, regulates cortisol, estrogen, testosterone, growth hormone, and thyroid hormone. The posterior pituitary releases oxytocin and vasopressin. Because oxytocin originates in the hypothalamus itself, a strong connection to appetite and eating disorders is anatomically inevitable. And indeed, electrical stimulation of the ventromedial nucleus of the hypothalamus — a region densely packed with oxytocin receptors — suppresses appetite, lending powerful support to the inference that oxytocin inhibits hunger. Furthermore, the simple act of eating activates oxytocin neurons in the hypothalamic-pituitary region, promoting oxytocin release and curbing further appetite. Put another way: when your stomach is full, your oxytocin rises — which neatly explains why people tend to become more generous after a meal.[18-3]

This insight changed how I run my own classroom. I now tell my students, without exception: never hold a group meeting on an empty stomach. Bring donuts, cookies — anything. When blood sugar drops, stress hormones climb. And if eating raises oxytocin, it suddenly makes perfect sense that the corporate world conducts its most important negotiations over food. A 2011 Columbia University study analyzed 1,112 judicial rulings in relation to meal timing and found that judges handed down significantly harsher sentences when hungry and far more lenient ones after eating. They returned to the bench after lunch as the most magnanimous people on earth. So here is my advice to couples: if your partner has been uncharacteristically prickly and short-tempered, take their hand — without a word — walk them to a good restaurant, look them in the eye, and ask softly, "Is something weighing on you?" You may just transform a miserable evening into an unforgettable one.[18-4]

Oxytocin: A Game-Changer for Dieting

A growing body of research confirms what intuition has long suspected: lonely people are hungrier, and under identical experimental conditions, they eat more than their socially connected counterparts. Some studies have implicated ghrelin, the appetite-stimulating hormone — researchers reasoned that because eating is fundamentally a communal act, loneliness drives ghrelin secretion to compel the isolated person toward food. But recent discoveries make clear that oxytocin is the deeper player in this drama. At Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, twenty-five healthy adult men inhaled oxytocin before a generous breakfast. Compared to the placebo group, they consumed 122 fewer kilocalories — and the reduction came specifically from fat. More remarkably still, oxytocin simultaneously lowered ghrelin secretion and reduced insulin resistance.[18-5]

A German study found that oxytocin cut chocolate cookie consumption by a full twenty-five percent. When participants ate standardized meals after oxytocin administration, their stress hormones, blood glucose, and leptin levels all fell — demonstrating therapeutic promise even for diabetes management. The findings were published in 2013 in Diabetes, the field's most prestigious journal. More recently, several research groups have raised the tantalizing possibility that oxytocin could be developed as an anti-obesity medication. The vicious cycle is now well mapped: loneliness depresses oxytocin and elevates stress hormones; stress hormones spike insulin resistance, drive blood sugar upward, and break down muscle to forge new glucose in the liver; meanwhile, depleted oxytocin unleashes ghrelin, which demands more food. It is a metabolic triangle of hell. And the escape route? An oxytocin-rich lifestyle. There is no need to chase diet programs that cost a small fortune. Want to be lean? Get your body making oxytocin.[18-6]

Oxytocin Lifestyle Tip: Schedule important meetings after meals, or at minimum, set out light refreshments. Nothing of consequence should be decided on an empty stomach.
