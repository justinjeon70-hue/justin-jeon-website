Part Five: Oxytocin for a Healthier You

Chapter 19: The Hormone That Keeps You Slim

-- Oxytocin, Obesity, and Diabetes

In 1995, when I began my master's program at the University of Alberta in Canada, I never in my wildest dreams imagined that I would one day be researching obesity. The main reason I went to Canada was to study adapted physical activity under Dr. Robert D. Steadward, who chaired the International Paralympic Committee and whom I had met during a student exchange. At that point, I had no idea how broad the field of adapted physical activity truly was. It encompassed exercise physiology, medicine, sociology, psychology, administration — even marketing. The deeper I went, the more I realized I couldn't fathom its full depth.

I chose exercise physiology, my greatest area of interest, and began my graduate studies in earnest while also prescribing exercise programs for individuals with various disabilities. My particular assignment was to apply electrical stimulation to the paralyzed muscles of people with spinal cord injuries, causing the muscles to contract and relax so they could perform cycling, walking, and rowing exercises. Then one day, I noticed that the patients I was working with all had noticeably large bellies. If they have this much abdominal fat, doesn't that raise their diabetes risk? Worried, I searched the literature using "spinal cord injury" and "diabetes" as keywords. Sure enough, individuals with spinal cord injuries had a diabetes risk three to five times higher than the able-bodied population. And yet there wasn't a single study on exercise for this population. From that point on, I seized on the idea that exercise could dramatically reduce diabetes risk by lowering insulin resistance, and I launched into diabetes research.[18-1]

From Diabetes to Obesity

I went on to publish a study showing that exercise improves insulin resistance and blood sugar regulation in individuals with spinal cord injuries. That led to a deeper question: Why are people with spinal cord injuries so prone to diabetes? I found my answer in a hormone called leptin. I started my doctoral program in 1998, and when leptin was first discovered in 1994, the mechanisms by which it regulated obesity were still unclear. Curious about why people with spinal cord injuries were heavier and more prone to diabetes, I conducted intensive research from the perspective of leptin. I was the first to demonstrate that leptin regulates basal metabolic rate through the autonomic nervous system — specifically the sympathetic nervous system — and that fasting and exercise influence leptin levels. I then pursued deeper leptin research at the Diabetes Center affiliated with Harvard University, where I studied the brain alongside Dr. Terry Maratos-Flier, focusing intensively on the hypothalamus, known as the brain's feeding center.[18-2]

In fact, the anterior pituitary, connected to the hypothalamus, regulates the secretion of cortisol (the stress hormone), estrogen, testosterone, growth hormone, and thyroid hormone. The posterior pituitary releases oxytocin and vasopressin. Given that oxytocin is produced in the hypothalamus, it's logical to expect a strong connection between oxytocin and eating disorders. Indeed, electrical stimulation of the ventromedial nucleus within the hypothalamus suppresses appetite — and this region is densely populated with oxytocin receptors, supporting the inference that oxytocin inhibits appetite. Furthermore, when we eat, oxytocin neurons in the anterior hypothalamus-pituitary region are activated, promoting oxytocin secretion and reducing appetite. In other words, when you're full, oxytocin goes up — which explains why people tend to become more generous after a meal.[18-3]

This insight changed how I run my own classes. I now tell my students: never hold group meetings on an empty stomach. Bring donuts, bring cookies — bring something. When you're hungry and blood sugar drops, stress hormones go up. And since eating raises oxytocin levels, it suddenly makes perfect sense why important business meetings in the corporate world are almost always conducted over a meal. In fact, a Columbia University study in 2011 analyzed 1,112 judicial rulings and found that judges handed down significantly harsher sentences when they were hungry and far more lenient ones after eating. Judges sat down at the bench as the most generous people in the world — right after lunch. So here's my advice to couples: if your partner is being unnecessarily prickly and irritable, take them by the hand — without a word — to a great restaurant, look them in the eye, and ask, "Sweetheart, is something bothering you?" You just might turn a date from hell into a date in heaven.[18-4]

Oxytocin: A Game-Changer for Dieting

A growing body of research shows that lonely people feel hungrier, and that lonely individuals eat more than control groups under identical experimental conditions. Some studies have pointed to ghrelin, the appetite-stimulating hormone, as a culprit — researchers reasoned that because eating is fundamentally a social activity, loneliness triggers ghrelin secretion to drive appetite. But in light of recent findings, we now understand that oxytocin is the real player behind these changes. A study conducted at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston found that when twenty-five healthy adult men inhaled oxytocin and were then served a generous breakfast, they consumed 122 fewer kilocalories compared to the placebo group. They specifically reduced fat intake. What's more, oxytocin lowered ghrelin secretion and reduced insulin resistance at the same time.[18-5]

Another study conducted in Germany found that oxytocin reduced chocolate cookie consumption by 25 percent. And when participants were given standardized meals, oxytocin lowered stress hormones, blood sugar, and leptin levels — showing promise even for improving diabetes. This study was published in 2013 in Diabetes, the world's leading journal in the field. More recently, several researchers have raised the possibility that oxytocin could be developed as an anti-obesity medication. The vicious cycle is clear: the lonelier you are, the lower your oxytocin drops and the higher your stress hormones climb. Stress hormones spike insulin resistance, raising blood sugar and breaking down muscle to generate new glucose in the liver. Meanwhile, low oxytocin boosts ghrelin production, which makes you eat more. It's a hellish triangle. And the way out? Raise your oxytocin through an oxytocin-friendly lifestyle. There's no need to chase expensive diet programs that cost hundreds of dollars. Want to be slim? Then get your body making more oxytocin![18-6]

Oxytocin Lifestyle Tip: Schedule important meetings after meals, or at least prepare light refreshments. Don't do anything important on an empty stomach.
