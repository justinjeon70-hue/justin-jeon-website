Part One: What Is Oxytocin?

Chapter 1: The Secret of Roseto

In 1882, eleven men and women from Roseto Valfortore — a small hilltop village in the Foggia province of southern Italy — boarded a steamship bound for New York. Eight of them, hearts swollen with hope, crossed the Atlantic and settled near Bangor, Pennsylvania. But the New World did not roll out the welcome mat. Earlier waves of Welsh and German immigrants had already claimed the territory and guarded it with collective hostility. Perhaps to escape the hostility, four of the eight eventually scattered to other parts of America. One got back on a ship and sailed home to Italy.

The returnee was greeted like a hero. His harrowing tales of the journey — the vast ocean, the alien cities, the fierce competition for survival — traveled from kitchen to kitchen until they assumed the proportions of myth. The stories planted a seed of longing in the villagers' hearts. Before long, the trickle became a flood. By 1894, more than 1,200 residents of Roseto Valfortore — essentially everyone except women and small children — had obtained American passports. An entire village was uprooting itself and crossing the Atlantic.

When these immigrants arrived in their strange new country, they settled beside Bangor and built their own community, safely removed from the territorial hostility of those who had come before. They named it Roseto, after the home they ached to remember. It became the first officially recognized Italian-immigrant municipality in the United States.

A Heart Doctor Takes Notice

This unremarkable immigrant-settlement story might have faded into obscurity. Instead, in 1960, it exploded into the international spotlight. Documentaries were produced in at least four countries. Physicians, sociologists, and psychologists descended on the village in droves. Every resident was subjected to blood tests, electrocardiograms, questionnaires, and in-depth interviews. From the results emerged a term that would enter the medical lexicon: the Roseto Effect. A tiny immigrant village had captured the attention of the world.

Why?

The story begins with a chance encounter between two doctors: Benjamin Falcone and Stewart Wolf. Wolf, an internist at an Oklahoma hospital, happened to be vacationing in eastern Pennsylvania when he met Falcone, who had spent seventeen years as the local physician serving both Roseto and Bangor. Over a conversation that neither man could have known would change medical history, Falcone shared a puzzling observation: "The people of Roseto die of heart disease before sixty-five at a rate far lower than their neighbors in Bangor. Isn't that strange? The two communities are practically on top of each other, yet their life expectancies are worlds apart."

Most people would have filed this away with a polite "How interesting" and moved on. Wolf did not. He secured research funding and launched a rigorous investigation.

He began by verifying Falcone's claim. Gathering local mortality data, he analyzed every death in Roseto between 1955 and 1961 and compared them with deaths in Bangor — a community that shared the same water supply and the same healthcare system. The results were staggering: the cardiovascular mortality rate in Roseto was less than half that of Bangor, and less than half the national average across the entire United States. More astonishing still, virtually no one in Roseto had died of heart disease before the age of sixty-five. Psychiatric hospital admissions among Roseto's residents were also less than half the American average.1

Some readers may be thinking: "Of course — they were Italian immigrants. They must have eaten a Mediterranean diet, drizzled olive oil on everything, and kept their weight down." If the explanation had been that simple, Roseto would never have made headlines. Wolf's findings told precisely the opposite story. The residents of Roseto carried every classic risk factor for heart disease. They ate abundant meat, shunned exercise, and drank and smoked with enthusiasm. Their blood pressure was indistinguishable from the national average. Far from anointing their food with olive oil, they cooked in lard. Their blood cholesterol was no different from that of Americans anywhere else in the country.

The genetic hypothesis was an obvious candidate. Roseto's residents had all emigrated from the same Italian village, and most had married within the community — so perhaps they shared a fortunate gene pool. To test this, Wolf tracked down people who had been born in Roseto but moved away as children. Their cardiovascular disease rates and mortality were identical to the American average. Their diets, exercise habits, fat consumption, smoking rates, and cholesterol levels matched those of the Roseto natives who had stayed behind. Genetics could not explain it. So what on earth was keeping the people of Roseto alive?

The answer revealed itself soon enough. "The most striking thing," Wolf later wrote, "was that the people of Roseto were enjoying their lives." They lived simply, modestly, without pretension. Crime was virtually nonexistent. Those who had more gave generously; those who had less accepted help without embarrassment. In a village of barely two thousand people, there were no fewer than twenty-two community organizations — fishing clubs, hunting groups, reading circles, sports teams. Many households sheltered three generations under a single roof, and communal celebrations were a constant. They lived, in effect, as one vast extended family. Wolf's conclusion was clear: it was this dense web of social connection — this closeness, this mutual care — that protected Roseto's hearts and granted its people long life.

Why the Roseto Effect Vanished

Are the people of Roseto still living long, heart-healthy lives today? The answer, sadly, is no. Wolf continued to follow the community for twenty-five more years and published his updated findings in 1989. The opening of his conclusion is haunting: "The most remarkable social change was the emergence of ostentation — something that had been taboo in Roseto for generations. The people had long believed that showing off attracted the devil's notice. But the younger generation abandoned this belief and began to compete in displaying their wealth. They drove expensive cars through the village streets, and a culture of petty comparison and rivalry took root."

The young people of Roseto left to seek work elsewhere. Newcomers filled their places. The once-intimate communal culture dissolved. Roseto became indistinguishable from Bangor next door, and the gap in cardiovascular mortality closed with it. The Roseto Effect vanished from the earth.2

In the decades since, as obesity rates have climbed worldwide, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and above all cancer have surged at a pace that can only be called alarming. Deaths from infectious diseases like pneumonia have declined, while deaths from metabolic and lifestyle-related diseases now account for more than 67 percent of all mortality. In this landscape, what should we focus on to live longer and better? Perhaps the answer is not only exercise and diet. Perhaps it also lies in seeing family and friends more often, staying socially engaged, and nurturing the relationships around us.3

Reading about Roseto, I was struck by a wave of recognition. This is the village where I grew up. Everyone was poor. Life was hard. But through traditions like dure and pumasi — communal labor-sharing customs that are Korea's own version of the barn-raising — people divided whatever they had, even if it was just half a soybean, and treated one another like kin. Neighbors slipped in and out of each other's homes so freely that everyone knew exactly how many spoons and chopsticks hung in every kitchen. When misfortune struck a family, the whole village wept alongside them. When joy arrived, it was celebrated as though it belonged to everyone. Weddings and funerals summoned the entire community. Fathers fussed over the ceremonial fruit and confections for a neighbor's ancestral rite as meticulously as for their own. Mothers set vast cauldrons in the yard and boiled enormous batches of noodles to feed whoever came.

Then, somewhere along the way, it all began to slip away. Three generations gathering for holidays became the exception, not the rule. Village paths were sliced open by straight paved roads. Homes retreated behind high concrete walls. People bolted their doors and stopped attending communal events. Grown children left for Seoul as if by silent agreement, and only the gray-haired remained, keeping vigil on empty verandas. The same erosion swept through the workplace: communal culture dissolved, face-to-face meetings migrated to screens, and the occasions for human contact grew scarcer by the year. Company dinners vanished. Even sharing a table with one other person began to feel like a burden. Honbap (eating alone), honsul (drinking alone), "I'm perfectly fine by myself" — solo living became a competitive performance. Turn on the television, and variety shows present it as the natural state of modern existence.

Is it any wonder that schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorder, and other mental illnesses have surged? Suicide rates have become impossible to ignore. South Korea holds the grim distinction of the highest suicide rate among all OECD nations. In 2022, it stood at 26 per 100,000 — 2.4 times the OECD average of 11. The trajectory is revealing: Korea's suicide rate began its relentless climb in the early 1990s, peaking at 31.7 per 100,000 in 2011. As the nation rode the so-called Miracle on the Han River through breakneck industrialization, every citizen was swept into a race without a finish line. Social polarization widened, relationships fragmented, and the pressures of college entrance exams and job-hunting reached suffocating intensity. Is there no way out?

From this vantage point, I find myself asking whether the "social hormone" oxytocin might hold part of the answer. Some readers may be skeptical: "Oxytocin? The hormone that surges during childbirth and sex? How could that have anything to do with our health or our future?" I once thought the same. But perhaps what Stewart Wolf glimpsed in the 1960s — when he compared the old Roseto with the new Roseto and with mainstream America, framing the difference as "competition versus cooperation" in his book The Power of Clan: The Influence of Human Relationships on Heart Disease — was only the tip of the iceberg.

Let us step into the world of oxytocin.
