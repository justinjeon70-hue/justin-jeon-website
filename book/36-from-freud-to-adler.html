Part Eight: The Power of Narrative

Chapter 36: From Freud to Adler

-- From the Amygdala to the Prefrontal Cortex

"Is physical activity good for depression?"

If you asked most people, the answer would be an unhesitating yes. But research recently published by my laboratory tells a more complicated story. Physical activity performed at work — occupational movement — actually increases the prevalence of depression by up to threefold as it rises. Leisure-time physical activity, by contrast, halves the risk. Another of our papers found that even sedentary time affects mental health differently depending on its purpose. Sitting while studying showed no association with depression or suicidal ideation. But increased time on mobile devices scrolling social media — especially among adolescent girls — was linked to a significant rise in depressive feelings and suicidal impulses. When we perform a behavior, the impact on our health depends on the value we assign to that behavior.1

How to Turn Labor into Exercise

There are two ways to make a laboratory mouse exercise: a treadmill and a running wheel. When researchers compared treadmill mice, running-wheel mice, and sedentary mice to see which group died first, the result was striking. The treadmill mice died earliest. Then the sedentary mice. The running-wheel mice survived the longest.

Why?

The treadmill was never the mouse's idea. A researcher placed the animal on the belt, chose the speed, and flipped the switch. The researcher also decided how long the mouse would run. If the mouse refused or faltered, an electric shock plate waited at the back of the belt. Worse still, mice are nocturnal creatures, but the humans running the experiment worked during the day — so the mice ran on human schedules, not their own. Translate this into human terms: imagine a drill sergeant barging into your barracks at two in the morning, marching you out to the parade ground in your underwear, and forcing you through calisthenics. Refuse, and you get beaten.

The running wheel was a different world. Those mice treated running as play. They chose when to start, how fast to go, and when to stop. If they felt like sprinting to a runner's high, they could. If they wanted to step off the wheel before breaking a sweat, no one stopped them. Run after eating, sleep instead of running — the choice was always theirs. No electric shocks. No missed meals. In one of my own studies, mice measuring just eight or nine centimeters ran over forty kilometers a week — farther than a round trip from Yonsei University's Sinchon intersection to Seoul National University at the foot of Gwanak Mountain. Force them to cover that distance and running ceases to be exercise. It becomes *labor. That is exactly the point. Physical activity at work is labor. Physical activity at leisure is joy.2 Even when the movement is identical, what matters is whether it was freely chosen.

Consider another example. A hundred people run a marathon. In general, the better your finish, the higher your post-race testosterone.* So does fiftieth place always produce lower testosterone than second? "It depends" is the honest answer. The runner in fiftieth might be completing their first marathon, ecstatic just to have finished, and delighted to learn fifty people came in behind them. The runner-up, meanwhile, might have spent a year in brutal training with the single goal of winning — and be consumed with frustration at falling short. Context and meaning determine how the same physical stress reverberates through mind and body.

At Stanford University, Alia Crum conducted a study during her graduate years at Harvard that would reshape the field. She told hotel housekeepers that their daily labor actually qualified as exercise, then surveyed how much exercise they believed they were getting and measured their weight and blood pressure. The housekeepers who received this reframing perceived themselves as exercising more — and their weight and blood pressure dropped significantly. Crum then went further. She placed two groups in equally stressful situations. One group was taught that stress is a force that helps us rise to challenges. The other was told that stress is toxic and should be avoided at all costs. When she measured their hormones, the group taught to see stress as useful showed greater positive emotion, enhanced cognitive flexibility, and higher levels of DHEA — a hormone that combats depression, bolsters immune function, and strengthens bones and muscles. The group taught that stress is purely harmful? None of these benefits appeared. "If you can't avoid it, enjoy it," the old saying goes. These experiments suggest the proverb is scientifically defensible. When we cannot escape a stressful situation, simply knowing that stress can be beneficial is enough to help us cope.3

The brain structures that respond first to our environment are the amygdala and the hippocampus. Running elevates the heart rate, quickens breathing, and triggers sweating — physiologically, it is a significant stressor for everyone. But whether running feels exhilarating or excruciating depends entirely on why you are doing it. During my military service, the compulsory group runs were agony. The reason was simple: I had no say in the matter, no idea when it would end, senior soldiers cursing behind me if I slowed, and the threat of ten extra laps if even one man fell back. Meanwhile, the senior leading the run was perfectly comfortable — he ran at whatever pace he liked, stopped whenever he chose, and could turn back to the barracks the moment his legs felt heavy. The amygdala reacts reflexively to bodily signals and environmental pressures — threats, coercion, danger. But it is the prefrontal cortex that provides context, assigns meaning, and enables an appropriate response rather than a raw reaction.

The architecture of the brain is telling. The amygdala sits right beside the hippocampus — because the emotions we feel about the people and situations we encounter need to be tagged and stored immediately. Threatening situations, dangerous people, hazardous places: remembering these things is how we survive. Animals with damaged amygdalae cannot react instantaneously to threats, and their survival rates in the wild plummet. Robert Sapolsky has recently reflected publicly that he spent his entire career studying stress and the hippocampus, when he should have been studying the prefrontal cortex all along. Because in the end, contextual interpretation of any given situation — what it means — is the prefrontal cortex's work. The prefrontal cortex matters. We must shift from the reptilian brain to the modern human brain. You can resolve a hundred times not to lose your temper, but when the trigger comes, anger fires anyway.

Freud saw trauma as the root of hysteria and believed healing required excavating the memories and emotions that caused it — tracing the connections between amygdala, hippocampus, and long-term memory to resolve the buried wound. This process — psychoanalysis — demanded long and laborious journeys back to the original pain. Adler took the opposite tack. Reject the trauma narrative, he counseled, and look forward. His view, distilled: stumbling blocks can become stepping stones.

From Freud to Adler

Let me illustrate with a figure almost every Korean knows — Moses, the protagonist of the Disney animated film The Prince of Egypt. Moses was born a Hebrew within the Egyptian empire. At the time, every Hebrew infant under one year of age was condemned to death. His mother, unable to kill her own child, placed her newborn in a reed basket sealed with pitch and set it adrift on the Nile. When an Egyptian princess plucked the basket from the river, Moses was raised in the palace as a prince of Egypt. A staggering reversal.

Then, around the age of forty, Moses killed an Egyptian official with his bare hands for brutalizing a Hebrew slave. Perhaps he imagined it was his destiny to champion his people — the self-consciousness of a would-be hero. But the next day, when he tried to break up a fight between two Hebrews, one of them snarled: "What — are you going to kill us the way you killed that Egyptian?" The murder had already spread through the grapevine. Terrified, Moses fled into the wilderness.

He ran for days without looking back. In the desert he met Zipporah, married her, and had a son he named Gershom — "I have become an alien in a foreign land." The name drips with self-pity and despair, a perfect distillation of where Moses' mind had gone. Then a second son arrived. This one he called *Eliezer — "God rescued me from the sword of Pharaoh." At the time of that naming, Moses' external situation had not changed one degree. He was still in the middle of nowhere, tending not even his own sheep but his father-in-law's flock — the very portrait of a man going nowhere. Yet from within that desolation, Moses attempted a completely new interpretation. A new context. A new meaning.

Shortly after naming his second son Eliezer, Moses saw a bush burning on Mount Sinai — burning but not consumed — and received from that fire the immense calling to lead the Hebrew people out of bondage.

Nothing in his circumstances had changed. But the moment he reinterpreted his past, a new future came into view. Moses had blamed the Hebrews who failed to appreciate his intentions. He had blamed Pharaoh. He had blamed God. But now he saw that every twist and calamity had been part of a larger design — the liberation of his people from slavery. With that understanding, his future and his purpose crystallized. If his first son Gershom represented the reflexive reaction to circumstance (the amygdala), then his second son Eliezer represented meaning discovered through contextual reinterpretation (the prefrontal cortex). This was not past-oriented psychoanalytic excavation (Freud) but future-oriented reframing of the past (Adler). When Moses' narrative changed, his wife changed, his family changed, his people changed, and the course of world history changed with them.

We must make the same shift — from Freud to Adler. Not the amygdala's reflexive firing but the prefrontal cortex's contextual reading and meaning-making: that is what bends our lives toward the future. Adler himself regarded his congenital disability and the chronic sting of being compared unfavorably to his brother as the very forces that propelled him to become a better person. Do not say, "I didn't receive enough love as a child, so my oxytocin is low, and that's why I can't be a good father or husband." Say instead — and believe it: "Despite all of that. No — because of all of that, I will love my children and my spouse more fiercely, and I will build a brighter, warmer home." Live the oxytocin lifestyle. Dream of a future overflowing with love.

Oxytocin in Action: Think back to the hardest moment of your life. Now assign it a new meaning. You may begin to see directions and possibilities you never noticed before.

\* The term "runner's high" was first used by the American psychologist Mandell in a 1979 paper, describing the euphoria experienced by dedicated runners.

\* In a state of overtraining, testosterone — which normally promotes muscle growth and repair — declines, while stress hormones rise.

\* "Gershom" conveys the sense: "I am an alien in a foreign land."
