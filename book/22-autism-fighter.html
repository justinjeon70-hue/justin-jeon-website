Part Five: Oxytocin for a Healthier You

Chapter 22: The Autism Fighter

--- Oxytocin as a Potential Treatment

Aristotle famously declared human beings zoon politikon — social, or political, animals. Most of us nod along at the first half of that formulation while feeling quietly uneasy about the second word: animal. In The Origins of Happiness, Professor Seo-eun Guk argues that happiness is not life's ultimate goal but rather a survival tool — that happy people outlast unhappy ones in the long struggle of existence. Professor Seo describes humans as "one hundred percent animal" and stages a provocative contest between Aristotle's metaphysical idealism and Darwin's evolutionary theory. The framing ignited debate. But his conclusion circles back to common ground: what matters most for human happiness is the sharing of emotions and thoughts with others, and the maintenance of meaningful, enduring relationships.

Reading the Mind in the Eyes

Emotion is among the most fundamental elements of our inner architecture. Before we are rational beings, before we are logical, we are creatures of feeling. When we cannot read our own emotions or decipher those of others, we fall into a state that might be called emotional illiteracy. The American psychotherapist Claude Steiner argued early on that just as literacy means the ability to read and write, emotional literacy means the capacity to perceive another person's emotions and express one's own in productive ways. An emotionally literate adult does not weaponize feelings, does not throw tantrums when emotions go unvalidated, does not demand comfort while refusing genuine dialogue. Such a person can channel emotion toward inner growth, conflict resolution, deeper affection, and a stronger sense of belonging.[21-1]

Karla McLaren, in The Language of Emotions, makes a complementary argument: a socially healthy human being is one who can read others' emotions and respond in kind. McLaren observes that modern culture — saturated by media messaging — has taught us to treat emotion as the chief saboteur of relationships, an inconvenience best suppressed. We learn to regard public displays of feeling as taboo, to project constant positivity, to keep our inner weather locked behind a serene facade — all while covertly surveilling the emotional states of those around us, like spies in our own lives. These myths, McLaren argues, collapse the moment we confront real emotional loss, communication failure, or mental illness.[21-2]

The German poet Christian Friedrich Hebbel once wrote: "The eye is the point where body and soul meet." The information conveyed by a human gaze is a dispatch from the deepest chambers of the self. And this is precisely the channel that autism disrupts. Children on the autism spectrum struggle to make eye contact, and without eye contact, the emotional world of others remains opaque. In 2001, Professor Simon Baron-Cohen developed the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RME Test) as a tool for identifying autism spectrum conditions — a deceptively simple assessment in which subjects view photographs of eyes alone and attempt to identify the emotion being expressed.[21-3]

Oxytocin Taught Them to Read Eyes

A fascinating recent study demonstrated that oxytocin can sharpen the ability to decode another person's emotional state. Healthy men aged twenty-one to thirty were tested at least one week apart — once after inhaling oxytocin, once after inhaling a placebo — and then administered the RME Test. Because the test was originally designed for individuals with autism and might prove too easy for neurotypical adults (a potential ceiling effect), the researchers divided the thirty-six items into easy and difficult categories. On the easy items, no significant difference emerged. But on the difficult items — the ones requiring genuine perceptual subtlety — participants who had inhaled oxytocin scored meaningfully higher. In plain terms: oxytocin sharpened their emotional radar.

The study was then extended to individuals on the autism spectrum. In 2013, a team at the University of Sydney recruited sixteen boys with autism spectrum disorder, aged twelve to nineteen, each with a mental age of at least twelve. One group inhaled oxytocin; the other received a placebo. Both then took the RME Test, with questions again separated by difficulty. The oxytocin group scored significantly higher overall, with the most dramatic gains on the easier items. On the harder items, the gap between groups narrowed. But the implication was unmistakable: children with autism had begun reading emotions from photographs of eyes — with oxytocin's assistance.[21-4]

Because no definitive treatment for autism spectrum disorder currently exists, these findings sent waves of excitement through the research community. Subsequent studies have tested oxytocin's effects across a broader range of autism presentations. Among the most promising results: facial recognition deficits are closely linked to oxytocin signaling, and oxytocin interventions have been shown to improve social relationships in individuals with high-functioning autism. These are genuinely stirring findings. It should be noted, however, that the field is still young, and some studies have found that inhaled oxytocin does not significantly improve social functioning in all autistic individuals. The relationship between oxytocin and autism remains an active area of debate. Yet the fact that genetic variants in the oxytocin receptor gene are associated with diminished social functioning and elevated autism risk makes it difficult to dismiss oxytocin's role. As awareness of adult autism grows, combining targeted oxytocin supplementation with an oxytocin-promoting lifestyle may offer a meaningful path toward symptom improvement.[21-5]
