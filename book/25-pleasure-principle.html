Chapter 25: The Pleasure Principle -- How Sex Raises Oxytocin

-- How Sex Raises Oxytocin

Fair warning: this chapter ventures into R-rated territory, so brace yourself.

One day in 1986, a rather unusual advertisement appeared in the student newspaper at Stanford University School of Medicine in San Francisco: "Volunteers wanted. Will be paid to masturbate." Students who responded to this somewhat embarrassing ad had to pass not only physical but also psychological screening. The most important qualification? The ability to reach orgasm through masturbation. A total of 13 women and 9 men made the cut. After two preliminary visits to the lab to acclimate, each participant returned on two separate occasions for the actual experiment. Here is how it worked: blood was drawn six minutes before masturbation (sample one), again at the moment of orgasm (sample two), and a third time while the orgasm was sustained. For women who experienced multiple orgasms, additional blood samples were collected at each peak to track changes in blood oxytocin levels.1

I confess — I find myself more curious about how much the participants were compensated than about the actual results. But putting that aside: while not every sexual encounter culminates in orgasm, this study was the first to explore how orgasm affects oxytocin levels in humans. The results were unambiguous. Sexual stimulation raised blood oxytocin levels, and oxytocin peaked at the moment of orgasm.

That same year, a parallel study was conducted in the United Kingdom with male participants only. This time, before participants began masturbating, they were asked to engage in sexual fantasy, and their oxytocin levels were measured afterward. The result? Sexual fantasy alone had no effect on oxytocin. But masturbation and ejaculation sent oxytocin levels skyrocketing — up to seven times higher than baseline — and those elevated levels remained at their peak for a full 10 minutes after ejaculation, only gradually returning to normal after about 30 minutes. Taken together, these two studies tell us something important: imagining sex does not raise oxytocin, but orgasm and ejaculation do — significantly.2

Sex: You Too?

To be honest, there is no study that has directly measured oxytocin levels during partnered intercourse. As you might imagine, conducting research in the middle of sex presents certain... logistical challenges. Nevertheless, given that masturbation and orgasm raise oxytocin, and that even listening to sexually arousing stories has been shown to increase oxytocin in women, it seems virtually certain that sex raises blood oxytocin levels. A healthy sexual relationship between partners is not just about chasing orgasm or ejaculation — it is about confirming love and trust, about two people becoming wholly one in body and mind. Research showing that hugging a partner raises oxytocin, combined with the well-known importance of foreplay — the tender touching and intimacy that precede intercourse — all point to a deep connection between sex, physical touch, and oxytocin.

A particularly elegant study explored this connection. Researchers first surveyed couples about how much they relied on each other for support. Then each partner spent about 10 minutes alone, after which the couple was reunited in the same room. They held hands for 2 minutes, watched about 5 minutes of a romantic film they had selected in advance, discussed the film for 2 minutes, and shared a 20-second hug. They were then observed for another 10 minutes apart. The findings? Couples who reported feeling close and mutually supportive had higher blood oxytocin levels. Women with higher oxytocin also had lower resting blood pressure.

A recent study of 15,629 Americans found that people who had sex 52 or more times per year (roughly once a week) had a 49 percent lower mortality rate compared to those who had sex once a year or less. Cardiovascular mortality was 21 percent lower, and cancer-related mortality was a staggering 69 percent lower. In another study of 495 heart attack survivors, those who resumed sexual activity at least once a week after recovery had a 10 percent lower risk of cardiovascular death and a 44 percent lower risk of death from other causes. While these studies did not measure oxytocin directly, I suspect that oxytocin mediated much of the positive health impact of regular sexual activity.3

Oxytocin: The Orgasm Enhancer

"Every day, women dream of an orgasm they will never forget." This evocative advertising tagline speaks to the importance of orgasm for women. The word "orgasm" derives from the Greek orgasmos, meaning "swelling organ," and refers to the peak of sexual arousal. Orgasm matters for men too. During sex, both men and women experience contractions of the pelvic floor muscles surrounding the genitals and anus, producing waves of intense, electric pleasure — this is orgasm.

According to one report, most men achieve orgasm through ejaculation, but fewer than 10 percent of women consistently do so through intercourse alone. While male orgasm tends to follow a relatively straightforward pattern, female orgasm involves a far more complex sequence of stages, varying widely by situation and individual. In the romantic comedy When Harry Met Sally, there is a legendary scene where Sally (Meg Ryan) fakes an orgasm with theatrical gusto at a New York deli. "Oh, God! Right there! Oh, yes. Yes. YES!" Her performance is so convincing that a woman at the next table tells the waiter, "I'll have what she's having." To this day, the exact seat where Sally performed her memorable demonstration is marked at Katz's Delicatessen in Manhattan.

But you do not need to order what Sally was having. You just need to raise your oxytocin levels. "So if I could eat food sprinkled with oxytocin, that would be the ultimate solution, right?" I am sure some readers are thinking exactly this. In fact, a study investigated whether men who inhaled oxytocin before sex experienced greater pleasure. The results were exactly what you might expect. Oxytocin did not affect sexual desire or erectile function, but it significantly enhanced the intensity of orgasm, interpersonal connection with the partner, and post-coital relationship satisfaction. Whether male or female, rather than blaming each other for missed orgasms, perhaps we should focus on nurturing each other during sex, maintaining continuous physical affection, and prioritizing the other person's pleasure. After all, raising each other's oxytocin through emotional conversation, warm hugs, and tender touch for women — and through massage and direct physical contact for men — creates a virtuous cycle. Oxytocin enhances sex, and sex enhances oxytocin, locked together like the ancient ouroboros serpent swallowing its own tail, where you cannot tell which is the head and which is the tail.4

What is especially interesting is the afterglow — that lingering state of bliss that persists for 30 minutes or more after intense lovemaking — during which blood oxytocin levels remain remarkably elevated. So here is my advice: after making love, do not be the first one to get up and walk away. Hold your partner close one more time.
