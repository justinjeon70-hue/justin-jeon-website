Part Two: Oxytocin for the Soul

Chapter 7: Loneliness Makes You Sick

During my years as an international student, whenever someone in our community fell ill, we would gather around them with a kind of instinctive urgency. "Being alone in a foreign country is hard enough," we would say. "Being alone and sick is unbearable." We visited. We cooked. We prayed. And the student who had been bedridden, groaning under the covers, would often rise with surprising speed — as if the mere presence of others had returned something essential to their body. For years, COVID-19 made such visits impossible, replacing human warmth with mandated distance. But in our culture, hearing that someone is unwell has always been a summons: you find the time, and you go.

The Destructive Power of Loneliness

The Japanese coined the word hikikomori to describe people who withdraw from society entirely, sealing themselves inside their homes for months or years. In Korea, the equivalent term is eundunhyeong oettori — "reclusive loner." A 2023 Korean government report surveyed fifteen thousand households of young adults aged nineteen to thirty-four and found that 2.4 percent qualified as recluses with no medical reason — no pregnancy, no disability — for staying home. Extrapolated to the broader youth population, that figure translates to roughly 244,000 people. In 2021, alarmed by the scale of the problem, the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family announced a support package offering up to 650,000 won per month — approximately five hundred U.S. dollars — in living expenses, medical costs, and educational support to help these young people re-enter society.

What is the difference between isolation and solitude? Isolation is the state of being alone against your will — and feeling the sting of it. Solitude is a state you choose, a deliberate retreat into your own company. Isolation breeds negative emotion; solitude nurtures positive growth. We feel lonely when we crave connection but experience it too seldom or too superficially. Isolation should be avoided wherever possible. Solitude, if you can learn to embrace it, should be savored.

The health consequences of loneliness are devastating. Social isolation has been likened to smoking fifteen cigarettes a day, or to drinking six glasses of soju — Korea's clear distilled spirit, each one as potent as a stiff cocktail. Loneliness raises the risk of premature death more than physical inactivity or obesity.1

Early research linking loneliness to cardiovascular disease found that unmarried men and women had a 50 percent higher rate of heart disease and an 18 percent higher mortality rate than their married counterparts. At the time, the connection was debated. It is debated no longer. Study after study has demonstrated that loneliness weakens the immune system, elevates cardiovascular risk, and increases both cancer incidence and cancer mortality. The evidence is now considered established fact. One study examined two hundred breast cancer patients and found that those who reported feeling lonely experienced significantly greater pain, deeper depression, and more crushing fatigue.2

People who feel lonely face elevated risk for a broad spectrum of diseases, and their mortality rates are considerably higher. For years, experts attributed this to familiar suspects — elevated stress hormones, chronic systemic inflammation, the poor lifestyle habits of people with no one watching. But a newer, more unsettling theory has gained traction: loneliness erodes the very social skills a person needs to escape it. The erosion deepens the isolation, the isolation deepens the erosion, and a vicious downward spiral carries the lonely person further from health and connection with every turn.3

Oxytocin: A Friend in Loneliness

What role does oxytocin play in combating this spiral? Experts increasingly agree that low oxytocin may be the trigger — the match that lights the fuse — by which loneliness translates into disease. The lonelier and more isolated a person becomes, the lower their oxytocin falls. And low oxytocin further weakens sociability, amplifies negative emotions, and undermines both the formation of new relationships and the maintenance of existing ones. This is not a virtuous cycle. It is a trap.

Do people with genetic mutations in their oxytocin receptors experience more loneliness? A 2009 German study confirmed that they do: those with receptor mutations reported significantly greater social loneliness and isolation. And because the partners of people with low empathy also tend to feel lonelier, oxytocin's reach extends beyond the individual. It shapes whether healthy relationships form and endure — which in turn affects disease prevalence and mortality in ways we are only beginning to quantify.4

The hopeful corollary is this: the more people you meet, the more meaningful relationships you cultivate, and the more you engage in activities of mutual support, the higher your oxytocin climbs. And that rising oxytocin will rescue you — from loneliness, and from the cascade of illness it leaves in its wake.

An Israeli research team invited sixty-three elderly participants to play a "mirror game" — a deceptively simple exercise designed to stimulate the brain's mirror neurons by having partners mimic each other's movements. Every single participant enjoyed the game so thoroughly that they forgot, at least for a while, that they had been lonely. And here is the elegant finding: the emotional shift correlated precisely with changes in salivary oxytocin. As oxytocin rose, loneliness fell. The effect varied, however, depending on how close and familiar participants felt toward their game partners. The closer the perceived bond, the more dramatic the oxytocin surge — and the more completely loneliness receded.5

Given all of this, I can say with confidence that the most powerful way to break the vicious cycle of loneliness and disease is to raise the oxytocin in our bodies. To anyone who says, "I'm just naturally introverted — I probably have a mutation in my oxytocin receptors — happiness was never in the cards for me — I'll just live alone and be done with it" — please, do not give up. Research shows that even people who are naturally reserved and introverted experience more joy and happiness when they do enjoyable things with other people.6
