Part Six: The Oxytocin Lifestyle -- Body and Senses

Chapter 27: Master Your Gut, Master Your Oxytocin

, Master Your Oxytocin

This may sound like a non sequitur, so bear with me. Earlier in the book, I explained how the Korean proverb "When your cousin buys land, your stomach hurts" turns out to be more physiologically literal than anyone expected. Well, one of the most electrifying frontiers in modern science is the gut microbiome — the vast, teeming civilization of bacteria living inside your intestines. If the human body is composed of roughly 30 trillion cells, the bacteria in your gut number approximately 38 trillion. Let that settle for a moment: more bacterial cells inhabit your intestines than human cells make up your entire body. And these 38 trillion organisms are not a monoculture — they comprise some 1,000 distinct species, a staggering ecosystem of diversity. Analyze their collective genome and you find roughly 100 to 150 times more genes than exist in the entire human genome. It raises a genuinely disorienting question: Am I the host, or am I the guest? All of these bacteria together weigh a mere 200 grams — less than a bar of soap. So why has this tiny nation within the nation sent the global scientific community into a state of sustained exhilaration? Let us find out.

A Shocking Revelation from Fat Microbiomes

My own fascination with the microbiome began with media coverage of the so-called "obesity bacteria." A landmark 2013 study published in Science solved one of the most persistent puzzles in metabolism: why, among genetically identical twins, one can be obese while the other stays thin. The answer was startlingly simple. Researchers transplanted microbiome samples from the feces of an obese twin and a lean twin into laboratory mice. The mice that received the obese twin's microbiome grew fat. Those that received the lean twin's stayed slim. When I first encountered this paper, I dismissed it — "Come on, that can't be right." Body composition is shaped by so many variables that I assumed the researchers had failed to control for something obvious.1

Then I read the paper properly. The experimental conditions were meticulous. And the sequel was even more startling. When the researchers housed the fat-microbiome mice and the lean-microbiome mice in the same cage, the fat mice began losing weight. "Wait — dieting can be this easy?" Reading further, the mechanism revealed itself with an almost comic elegance. Laboratory mice do not have designated bathrooms. They defecate wherever they happen to be, step in each other's droppings, and — this being mice — eat them too. Through this indelicate process, the lean mice's microbiome migrated into the obese mice's guts, and the obese mice slimmed down. If this principle could be harnessed commercially, the multi-billion-dollar diet industry might find itself upended overnight.

Autism and the Microbiome

But even more compelling than the obesity research was the emerging connection between the microbiome and autism. Numerous studies have investigated whether antibiotic use contributes to autism, yet the scientific community remains evenly split, and no consensus has emerged. I find this entirely predictable. Autism has both genetic and environmental origins, and disentangling them is devilishly complex.*

The defining feature of autism spectrum disorder is impaired social functioning. As I discussed earlier, individuals with autism often cannot sustain eye contact, struggle to decode emotions from facial expressions, and even when they glimpse an emotion, have difficulty feeling it with the other person. Given what we know about oxytocin's relationship to autism — low oxytocin levels and mutations in the oxytocin receptor are both associated with diminished empathy and elevated autism risk — the gut connection becomes deeply relevant. Multiple genes governing the oxytocin receptor have been identified, and the number of variants an individual carries can help predict symptom severity.

So why might antibiotics affect autism? Because they devastate the microbiome. Approximately 70 percent of individuals with autism spectrum disorder have gastrointestinal problems. A research team in Arizona devised an ambitious protocol: they first treated children with autism with a two-week course of antibiotics, then transplanted healthy microbiome samples over seven to eight weeks and observed the outcomes.

The results were, in a word, stunning. Gastrointestinal symptoms improved by roughly 80 percent, and autism symptoms showed meaningful improvement as well. Two years later, the same team published a follow-up tracking the transplant recipients. Of the 18 children who participated, a striking number had seen their autism symptoms either vanish entirely or soften to mild levels. Fecal analysis of 16 of the 18 participants revealed substantial increases in Bifidobacterium and Prevotella — two genera of beneficial gut bacteria. The implication is extraordinary: altering the gut microbiome alone produced dramatic improvements in both digestive health and social functioning in children with autism.2

\* Genetic factors account for only about 10-20 percent of autism spectrum disorder. In cases where autism arises from environmental or acquired triggers, genetic predisposition plays a facilitating role. For instance, an individual who already carries numerous autism-related genetic variants cannot have their condition attributed solely to antibiotic use. Conversely, in the rare case where no such genetic variants are present, antibiotics alone might theoretically trigger symptoms — though this remains scientifically unverified.

Change Your Life to Master Your Gut

A natural question follows: if microbiome changes can improve social functioning in children with autism, could they do the same for the rest of us — for anyone whose social skills could use a lift? Several Korean university hospitals are already investigating this through selective clinical trials. But we do not all need fecal transplants. We can transform the ecology of our gut through what we eat three times a day.

The foods that nourish a flourishing microbiome are refreshingly ordinary. Gut bacteria feed primarily on carbohydrates — specifically, the kind that survive the small intestine and reach the colon intact. These are vegetables and dietary fiber. This is precisely why increasing vegetable intake matters so much. Second, reduce processed food. Your gut harbors both beneficial and harmful bacteria, and maintaining their balance is paramount. Processed foods starve the good bacteria and feed the bad, because they contain virtually no fiber. A diet rich in processed food and sugar destabilizes the microbial ecosystem, potentially fueling chronic inflammation and weakening the immune system.

Third, overhaul your lifestyle. Regular exercise, moderation in alcohol, no tobacco, adequate sleep, and a healthy weight all contribute to a thriving gut environment. Among exercise options, include activities that get you genuinely breathless — brisk walking, cycling, strength training. Choose exercises appropriate for your joints, and for oxytocin's sake, exercise with others whenever possible. Alcohol and smoking are catastrophic for gut health. This is so self-evident that I will spare you the lecture.

Adequate sleep and time-restricted eating are non-negotiable. These matter enormously because irregular eating habits — particularly the Korean tradition of lavish yasik (late-night snacking) — wreak havoc on both the gut and sleep. Consider the chemical formula for glucose: C6H12O6. Glucose is, in essence, carbon atoms with water molecules attached. When you wake in the small hours after a carb-heavy nighttime snack, it is because the water liberated during carbohydrate metabolism is demanding to be expelled. And carbohydrates raise blood insulin, which triggers wakefulness and fractures sleep architecture. Is protein a better late-night option? Not really. Protein demands far more digestive energy, forcing the stomach and intestines into overtime when they should be resting — making a heavy protein snack potentially even worse.3

Oxytocin Boost: Reduce spicy, salty, and processed foods. Build your diet around vegetables, fruits, and fiber. If a total overhaul feels daunting, commit to just one genuinely healthy meal a day. Small revolutions compound.

The Microbiome-Oxytocin Axis

The mechanisms by which the microbiome and oxytocin shape social behavior are remarkably parallel. Antibiotics that decimate gut bacteria cause oxytocin levels to drop, and sociability declines in tandem. Among beneficial gut bacteria, Lactobacillus reuteri stands out: it not only stimulates oxytocin production but also enhances social behavior. And the resemblance between L. reuteri's benefits and oxytocin's is almost uncanny — anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, antidepressant, immune-boosting, anti-obesity, and wound-healing properties. The overlap is too precise to be coincidence.

So what happens when you administer Lactobacillus reuteri to animals that lack functional oxytocin receptors? The results are telling. Without working oxytocin, Lactobacillus's wound-healing, immune, and anti-obesity effects collapse — dramatically reduced or abolished entirely. And when the vagus nerve — the superhighway of the parasympathetic nervous system — is severed, both oxytocin's and Lactobacillus's effects plummet in parallel. These findings dovetail with recent research demonstrating that stimulating the parasympathetic nervous system through meditation, exercise, and even targeted electrical stimulation can influence depression and a range of other psychiatric conditions. The conclusion is hard to escape: master your gut, and you master your oxytocin. That is not hyperbole. It is physiology.4
