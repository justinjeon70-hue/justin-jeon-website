Part Seven: The Oxytocin Lifestyle -- Mind and Community

Chapter 34: Forget Cortisol -- Choose the Oxytocin Lifestyle

-- Choose the Oxytocin Lifestyle

Stress keeps us alive.

Wait — stress keeps us alive? Is that not exactly backward? Is stress not the thing that kills us? In biological terms, stress itself is not the enemy. Chronic stress is the enemy. Acute stress, the kind that arrives in a flash and departs just as quickly, is the reason our species survived long enough to build civilizations.

Professor Robert M. Sapolsky, the world's leading authority on stress, explains this beautifully in Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers. Zebras spend their lives being chased by lions. They experience ferocious bursts of stress. And yet they never develop stomach ulcers. Why? Consider what happens physiologically when a zebra sees a lion charging.

In a life-or-death emergency, the body ruthlessly triages. Reproductive function? You are seconds from becoming a meal — mating can wait. Digestion? You are about to be someone else's dinner — your own can be postponed. Immune function? Growth? These are luxuries for creatures who survive the next sixty seconds. What matters right now is flooding the leg muscles with blood. Every other organ is cut off. Focus. Concentrate. To achieve this, heart rate rockets and blood pressure spikes.

The conductor of this emergency orchestra is cortisol, the stress hormone, along with adrenaline and the full activation of the sympathetic nervous system. Once the zebra escapes, the system reverses: stress hormones and adrenaline drop, the parasympathetic nervous system takes the wheel, and blood begins flowing to the organs again.1

Eustress and Distress

Hans Selye, the Canadian endocrinologist revered as the father of stress science, argued in The Stress of Life that the right amount of stress can be a genuine source of vitality. At the University of Montreal, he developed the "general adaptation syndrome" — a three-stage model of the stress response. In the alarm stage, the adrenal glands release adrenaline and cortisol, quickening the breath, ramping up perspiration, and sharpening anxiety. If the stressor persists, hormonal defenses mobilize to resist it (the resistance stage). When the stressor finally passes, the parasympathetic nervous system engages, ushering in relaxation, rest, and recovery (the exhaustion stage). Selye demonstrated clinically that when stress is not discharged promptly, it can become a lethal poison to the body.2

But Selye made a second, more surprising claim: stress, properly channeled, can enrich rather than destroy a life. He coined "distress" for the toxic variety and "eustress" for the energizing kind. Eustress, he argued, is the butterflies before a first kiss, the electric anticipation before a championship match or a concert, the thrill of mapping out an overseas journey. These experiences do not deplete us — they charge us with life. Distress, on the other hand, arises from relentlessly unpleasant stimuli: bereavement, work overload, chronic quarreling, the neighbor's late-night noise. Selye's prescription was to break stress into manageable intervals punctuated by rest, to shift one's perspective, and to cultivate more eustress. Do this, he insisted, and stress ceases to be the root of all disease. It becomes, instead, one of life's most unexpected gifts.3

Modern humans will not encounter a tiger or a lion on the sidewalk. Barring a zoo escape, the probability is effectively zero. (Though the recent wave of random public knife attacks has introduced a new and horrifying form of street-level threat.) So where does a modern person experience the kind of existential terror a zebra feels when a lion appears? At home. At school. At work. In the social world.

When a student is bullied or crushed by the pressure to maintain a place within a peer group, cortisol surges. Research suggests that prolonged stress during adolescence can actually suppress physical growth. For adults, the greatest stressors are likely the relentless performance demands of the workplace and the minefields of office relationships: pressure from above, competition from peers, envy, sabotage, the slow erosion of being undervalued — each of these can produce a hormonal response indistinguishable from a zebra's encounter with a lion.

The critical difference: a zebra's terror lasts seconds, perhaps minutes. Ours can last months. Years. If workplace stress can be discharged through a warm home, through hobbies and friends and weekend adventures, the system works as nature intended. But if there is no one at home to lean on, no friend with whom to share a therapeutic round of gossip about the terrible boss, no community or recreational outlet — that is not merely unfortunate. It is a serious threat to health: physical, psychological, and social.

The secret to converting distress into eustress is to install the oxytocin lifestyle as the operating system of your daily existence. You must find ways — right now, today — to pump oxytocin through your body. When you do, cortisol will fall. And life will begin to feel different.

The Stress Assassin: Oxytocin

Stress and oxytocin are bound together more intimately than most people realize. The brain structures that secrete stress hormones and those that release oxytocin sit side by side — anatomical neighbors sharing a fence. In a healthy body, when stress arrives, oxytocin rises alongside cortisol, buffering the stress response and coaxing cortisol back down. But when cortisol surges without a corresponding rise in oxytocin, the individual is left exposed to the full destructive force of chronic stress — physical, mental, and social.

This is why the oxytocin lifestyle is not a luxury but a necessity. However much we tell ourselves that stress brings productive tension and creative energy, stress that exceeds our personal threshold and goes unaddressed is a slow-moving catastrophe. Chronic stress is the root of all disease.

We must rebuild our lives around the oxytocin lifestyle. When stress finds you at school or work, reach for a friend immediately — share a meal, talk it out, let the words carry the weight away. On weekends, play outdoor sports together. Linger at a cozy cafe over brunch and let the gossip about your boss flow freely and without guilt. Join clubs. Pursue hobbies. Let the stress drain from your body. If no friends come to mind, do not surrender — visit your parents and give them a massage. Sign up at a neighborhood sports club. If exercise is not your thing, try a community lecture series or a book club — any gathering where you can meet new people and feel the warmth of shared experience.

And there is one more element of the oxytocin lifestyle that deserves mention: spiritual community. Walk into a nearby church, clap your hands, sing, listen to a sermon, enjoy the hearty communal lunch that follows. If church is not for you, try a Buddhist temple. Listen to the dharma talk, eat the temple food. And if you are there already, a gentle hike afterward — enough to break a sweat — makes everything better.

The most important insight — which I will explore more fully in the next chapter — is learning to separate what you can change from what you cannot in the circumstances that stress you. What you can change, change now. What you cannot change, accept or actively seek alternatives. Suppose your boss bullies you daily and leaving the job is not an option. Consider making an ally of your antagonist. There is a line in scripture: "Use worldly wealth to gain friends for yourselves" (Luke 16:9). In other words, heap burning coals on your enemy's head — with kindness.

I lived this lesson myself. In graduate school, there was a colleague who needled me constantly — passive-aggressive jabs delivered with a smile that made my blood pressure spike. "Maybe I should just deck him," I thought, briefly and darkly. Then I changed my mind. That Christmas, I gave him a modest gift with a carefully written, heartfelt card. From that day forward, he was a different person. He became one of my closest friends, and we remain in touch to this day.

Such outcomes are not common, I grant you. But when you are trapped in a stressful situation, doing something positive — something that sends oxytocin rather than cortisol into your veins — can alter the entire trajectory. This is the oxytocin lifestyle in its simplest form. Live this way long enough, and your attitude will shift, your environment will change, and even the circumstances that once tormented you will begin to transform.

Oxytocin Stress Management A life free of stress is a fantasy — and the attempt to avoid all stress is itself a source of stress. Perhaps the goal is not to eliminate stress but to manage it. How? First, approach as much of your life as possible with a spirit of willing engagement. There is no need to court stress deliberately. When you do something because you love it, the pressure may be real, but it carries a charge of joy. When you are forced to do what you despise, when your efforts go unrecognized, when the work itself becomes the wound — that is the worst. If you listed every task in your life, the ideal ratio of "things I want to do" to "things I must do" is roughly 7 to 3. Doing only what you want is not possible — and would be childish if it were. But when the ratio inverts to 3 to 7, productivity falls and stress climbs inexorably. Short stretches of imbalance are unavoidable; sometimes you simply must endure. When you cannot lower stress, raise oxytocin. When stress hits, do not cut contact and retreat into solitude, eating instant food alone in a darkened room — that path tanks your oxytocin and destroys body and mind together. The greater the stress, the more urgently you need the oxytocin lifestyle. Meet friends. Talk. Explore good restaurants. Go to a bathhouse and get a scrub and a massage. Participate more vigorously in clubs and communities. This is how you wrestle cortisol back down and keep yourself whole.
