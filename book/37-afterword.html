Afterword: The Health of Our Nation Is at Risk

The World Health Organization defines health through three pillars: physical, mental, and social. On all three fronts, the warning lights are flashing.

Physically, hypertension, diabetes, and cancer continue their relentless climb. Inflammatory bowel disease is surging. On the mental health front, depression and bipolar disorder have become almost ordinary diagnoses, while schizophrenia, anxiety disorders, sleep disorders, and obsessive-compulsive disorder are rising at alarming rates. And socially, the picture is perhaps most troubling of all. The rise of single-person households, declining marriage and birth rates, and the aging of the population have produced an epidemic of isolation — elderly people living alone, dying alone, their passing unnoticed for days or weeks. For more than a decade, South Korea has held the grim distinction of the highest suicide rate among OECD nations. Our television screens overflow with programs depicting marital conflict and generational estrangement. Tensions between neighbors, between regions, between genders — and now, layered atop all of this, the social distancing mandated by COVID-19. The health of our nation is under siege from every direction.

Are we truly happy right now?

Let me share something that happened long ago. One day I scolded the senior student representative in my research lab. What I hoped was that this student would rally the team: Let's pull ourselves together and get the work done. What happened instead was that the student I had reprimanded turned around and lashed out at fellow students, assigning blame and radiating frustration. The lab atmosphere, predictably, collapsed. I told myself I had been rational and measured in my criticism. The truth was uglier: I was drowning in my own workload and had simply vented my irritation. The student happened to be standing in the path of the blast. It became one of the worst precedents in my memory — my pointless eruption rippling outward. Those graduate students may well have gone home that evening and redirected their anger at parents, siblings, or partners, tangling yet more relationships in the process.

This is how human life works. We do not exist in isolation. We live enmeshed in relationships, and our emotions are ferociously contagious. Spend time with someone who is happy, and happiness seeps into you. Spend time with someone mired in depression, and a heaviness settles over you too. Surround yourself with someone whose oxytocin runs high, and your own levels climb. Live alongside someone whose cortisol is chronically elevated, and your own cortisol begins to spike and crash in sympathy.

Now imagine the reverse. A person with naturally high oxytocin joins your workplace. This person listens attentively to supervisors, trusts colleagues, and radiates kindness. They share food, maintain warm eye contact during conversations, and consistently consider others before themselves. When a gem like this enters a team, the oxytocin levels of everyone in the department would surge. Even when crises hit or unexpected obstacles arise, work would feel lighter. And the ripple would not stop at the department walls — it would spread to other divisions, to those employees' families, to partner companies, outward and outward.

This is not wishful thinking. Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler, authors of Connected, tracked more than 12,067 individuals across over 50,000 social connections for years. They demonstrated that obesity does not just spread to friends — it spreads to friends of friends, and even to friends of friends of friends. If every reader of this book were to practice the oxytocin lifestyle at home, the positive influence would radiate not only to spouses, parents, friends, and colleagues, not only to the stranger you meet on the street today, but outward through the groups and communities you belong to, and ultimately into the fabric of society itself. This is not arithmetic growth. It is exponential.

Recently I sat down with Professor Seo Eun-guk, author of The Origin of Happiness, and we spent a long evening discussing the relationship between extraversion and happiness, weaving in everything I had learned about oxytocin. He admitted, with a shrug and a smile, that he had not been aware of all the connections between oxytocin and social relationships, physical health, and mental well-being explored in this book. But, he added, he was not the slightest bit surprised that oxytocin was involved in all of them. Because oxytocin is the hormone of cheug-eun-ji-sim (compassion for the suffering of others), yeok-ji-sa-ji (the ability to stand in another's shoes), and i-sim-jeon-sim (heart-to-heart understanding without words) — three Confucian ideals that have guided Korean life for centuries.

Whether you explain this through social evolution — that kind individuals survived because their kindness secured help from others when they most needed it — or through a theology of design — that we were made to love and care for one another, and therefore know true happiness only when we do — the core truth remains unchanged. Oxytocin stands at the center of it all. Which of these two worldviews is correct lies beyond the scope of this book. But imagine a nation where more and more people carry high oxytocin, and more and more of them share its power with those around them. How much more beautiful and joyful would that nation be?

Carrying this vision, I — as a researcher devoted to health — began thinking years ago about how to improve both individual well-being and community flourishing through changes in lifestyle. That search led me to oxytocin. It was a kind of serendipity: my doctoral research in Canada had focused on the endocrine system, and my postdoctoral work at Harvard centered on the feeding center, the hypothalamus, and the pituitary gland — all of which gave me a strong foundation for understanding this remarkable hormone. As I read paper after paper on oxytocin, I could not help but fall under its spell. What first astonished me was the discovery that oxytocin is fundamentally a hormone of sociality and connection. What astonished me a second time was its deep entanglement with the diseases of modern life.

"Let Us Meet, Share, and Love — Earnestly."

Oxytocin is produced when we meet people, look into each other's eyes, talk, touch, and love. When it runs low, we do not merely become unhappy — we become ill. The painful truth is that the people of this country are gradually losing the art of meeting, conversing, and loving. Chronic disease and mental illness are climbing steeply. The COVID-19 pandemic turned isolation into policy, and as hyper-aging and automation advance in tandem, ordering a bowl of soup at a restaurant now means tapping a kiosk screen, and having noodles delivered to your door is an entirely contactless affair. It is heartbreaking. The enormous popularity of the Korean variety show I Live Alone tells us how many people recognize themselves in a solitary existence. The coining of the word untact — contactless — signals that we are standing at the far edge of a dystopian inheritance in which meeting another human being face to face has become awkward.

And yet. All life and all happiness begin with human touch. No matter how far society races into social networks, the metaverse, and the dazzling frontier of artificial intelligence, one truth will not bend: every meeting and relationship rooted in our physical bodies is what gave rise to the vast structure we call civilization. In the film Ready Player One, the dying Dr. Halliday offers his final counsel to the protagonist lost in a virtual world: "Go back to reality. That's the only place where you can get a decent meal." In an age that has moved beyond the nuclear family into the era of the "nuclear individual," we must rewrite the narrative of connection — the oxytocin story — if we are to build healthier people and happier communities.

Close this book. Right now. Call up a friend you have not seen in far too long and drag them out for a drink. Bring a plate of pajeon — Korean scallion pancakes — to a neighbor you have grown awkwardly distant from. Take your spouse on a proper date downtown, the kind you have not had since the two of you drifted into sleeping in separate rooms. I want to see that.

In the six months I spent writing this book, my own oxytocin was almost certainly higher than it has ever been. I noticed it only in retrospect: I was listening more attentively to my elders, looking more carefully into the eyes of the people I spoke with, treating my family with greater tenderness, holding them more often, showing more care and patience to colleagues and students. They say that knowledge is power, and that you see as much as you know. My deepest hope is that this book widens your view — even slightly — and helps make this wonderful country of ours a happier place to live.

And I pray that this book — even if it amounts to no more than a speck of dust, even if it is as small as a single mustard seed — plants something that grows toward a healthier, more loving nation.

Thank you.
