Chapter 31: Lock Eyes, and It Goes Up -- The Gift Your Dog Gives You

, and It Goes Up — The Gift a Dog Gives You

Oxytocin and Family Health Oxytocin is the quiet sentinel that guards a family's well-being. A loving embrace between spouses does more than feel good — it lowers the partner's blood pressure, curbs excessive appetite, and dials down stress, because that tender hug stimulates oxytocin release. The oxytocin that floods the body in response makes each partner more generous, more loving, drawing the marital bond tighter still. Hugging between parents and children raises oxytocin too. When those embraces carry spoken words of gratitude and love, oxytocin climbs further, calming the child's amygdala, firing up the prefrontal cortex, and sharpening memory, concentration, reasoning, and the ability to solve problems. I once had a friend who insisted on eating dinner at home every single night, practically bolting for the door the moment the workday ended. I resented being left behind at the time. Now I think he was wiser than any of us. When a family gathers around the table for a shared meal, oxytocin levels rise. Family dinners are best filled not with nagging or complaint but with expressions of gratitude — though celebrity gossip works perfectly well, and so does ordinary talk about the day. Conversation about whatever is on television is fine too. If the whole family can sing together in a choir or play in a band, wonderful. But that requires at least two or three musically inclined members. When the ensemble is not there, a trip to the local karaoke room after dinner — belting out songs together at the top of your lungs — works beautifully. And raising a dog as a family, with everyone's wholehearted agreement, is another powerful way to boost everyone's oxytocin. This requires careful deliberation, proper training, and thorough knowledge about caring for an animal. It is also essential that the dog's temperament and the family's personality align — otherwise, you may end up raising cortisol instead of oxytocin.

In 2015, a paper appeared in Science that stopped me in my tracks: simply gazing into your dog's eyes can trigger oxytocin release. Speaking for myself, one of the most quietly perfect moments in life is sitting beside a dog, stroking its fur, and meeting its gaze.

When I was a boy in Korea, dogs lived outside, tethered to a post all day. Their job was to guard the house, nothing more. Dog food did not exist — dogs ate whatever the family scraped from its plates. A "companion animal" was a concept that would have drawn blank stares. After my parents moved to an apartment and I left for graduate school overseas, I went decades without a dog. Then, five years ago, my younger son launched his campaign. He wanted a dog with a longing so fierce it bordered on the operatic. My wife, who harbored a deep aversion not merely to dogs but to animals in general, considered the proposition roughly as appealing as an earthquake.

But no parent ultimately wins a war of attrition against a determined child. Our second son escalated his siege to levels that would have impressed a medieval general, and as my wife's defenses crumbled, we happened to watch a Korean television program called Dogs Are Wonderful. The show tilted something in us: raising a dog, it argued, teaches children responsibility while offering a kind of emotional bond no other experience can replicate. We extracted from our son the solemn vow that he would walk the dog twice a day, every day, and adopted a puppy. That is how "Gureum" — Cloud — joined our family. Every walk and act of care, of course, promptly became my wife's job and mine. I loved Cloud from the first day. The way he came scampering to the door when I got home from work, tail whipping back and forth in ecstatic circles — it was a joy entirely different from raising two boys, and no less profound.

How Eye Contact Raises Oxytocin

When Cloud was a puppy, he would press his body against my side, begging to be petted, yet persistently avoid my eyes. Do dogs simply not make eye contact? I wondered, and thought no more of it. But as Cloud grew, he began to hold my gaze. When we sat together and looked into each other's eyes for a long, unhurried moment, something passed between us — wordless, unmistakable, as close to a conversation as silence can get.

Then I read the Science paper and understood the mechanism behind the magic. Dr. Miho Nagasawa brought 30 dogs and their 30 owners into a laboratory, allowed them to interact freely, and measured their oxytocin levels. Nagasawa had already demonstrated in 2009 that the deeper the bond between dog and owner, the longer the two would hold mutual gaze. This new study tested a more daring hypothesis: that oxytocin itself was the chemical currency of that bond and that gaze.

The results were elegant. The longer the eye contact between dog and owner, the higher the owner's oxytocin climbed. And the effect was not one-directional: the dogs' oxytocin rose in lockstep with their owners'. What made the study genuinely startling, though, was the control. When the identical protocol was conducted with wolves and their handlers, oxytocin did not budge. Something unique had happened in the evolutionary journey from wolf to dog — a hijacking of the same hormonal pathway that binds human parents to their infants. More recently, researchers have demonstrated that oxytocin is even involved when dogs shed tears upon reuniting with their owners after separation.1

To confirm that the human oxytocin spike was specifically driven by the dog's gaze, the researchers artificially elevated the dogs' oxytocin and then observed what happened. The prediction held perfectly: dogs with boosted oxytocin held eye contact with their owners for longer, and the owners' oxytocin levels rose in turn. This finding dovetails with earlier work showing that when fathers inhaled oxytocin, they spent more time gazing at their children — and the children's oxytocin rose too.

We all have the experience of meeting certain people and walking away feeling warmer, more energized, more human — and of meeting others who leave us drained, irritable, regretting the encounter entirely. Could the invisible hand behind these vastly different aftereffects be oxytocin? It is a question worth sitting with. MRI studies have shown that the same brain networks governing emotion, reward, and attachment light up when a mother sees a photograph of her child and when she sees a photograph of her dog. Perhaps our companion animals have crossed some threshold and become genuine members of the family. Nagasawa's findings hint that therapy dogs may offer meaningful help to people living with autism, PTSD, depression, and anxiety disorders.2

The Gift a Dog Gives You

One afternoon, I delivered a lecture on oxytocin to a group of retired and current senior professors at Yonsei University. Afterward, over lunch, one of the professors approached me. "I think you are right, Professor," he said. "I adopted a dog that had been returned by a previous owner. At first, she would not look me in the eye. But once she began sleeping in my bed, she started holding my gaze — as if she had decided to trust me. And she stopped barking at other dogs too."

It is possible, of course, that the dog simply grew calmer as she settled into a loving home. But given the research showing that raising a dog's oxytocin increases its loyalty and sociability toward its owner, the professor's story carries real weight. And when I watch people who can barely utter a word to strangers striking up easy conversation with fellow dog walkers at the park, I cannot help but wonder: do dog lovers walk through the world with higher baseline oxytocin?

Everyday Oxytocin Boost: Tonight, sit down and truly look into the eyes of the dog who has been waiting for you all day. If you do not have a dog, this might be the moment to consider adopting one. But please — only after careful thought, proper preparation, and a genuine understanding of what it means to share your life with another living creature.
