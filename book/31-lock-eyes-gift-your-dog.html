Chapter 31: Lock Eyes, and It Goes Up -- The Gift Your Dog Gives You

, and It Goes Up — The Gift Your Dog Gives You

Oxytocin and Family Health Oxytocin is the guardian that protects family health. A loving hug between spouses lowers the partner's blood pressure, suppresses excessive appetite, and reduces stress — all because that affectionate embrace stimulates oxytocin release. Elevated oxytocin makes couples more generous of spirit, heightens feelings of love, and deepens the marital bond. Hugging between parents and children also raises oxytocin. When hugs are accompanied by expressions of gratitude and love, oxytocin rises further, stabilizing the child's amygdala, activating the prefrontal cortex, and enhancing memory, concentration, reasoning, and problem-solving abilities. I once had a friend who insisted on eating dinner at home no matter what, practically sprinting home every evening. At the time, I resented being abandoned. Now I think he was a thousand times right. When the whole family sits down together for a meal, oxytocin levels go up. During family meals — as long as you are not nagging or complaining — expressions of gratitude are great, celebrity gossip is fine, and just chatting about the day works too. TV talk is perfectly acceptable. Having the whole family sing together in a choir or play in a band is wonderful. But that requires at least two or three musically inclined family members. If that is not possible, going to a karaoke room after dinner and belting out songs together works just as well. And raising a dog together, with everyone's agreement, is another excellent way to boost the family's oxytocin. Of course, this requires careful deliberation, proper training, and sufficient knowledge about dog care before adoption. It is also important to match the dog's temperament with the family's characteristics — otherwise, you might end up raising stress instead of oxytocin.

In 2015, a truly fascinating paper was published in Science: simply making eye contact with your dog can stimulate oxytocin release. Personally, one of the most deeply satisfying moments in life is petting a dog while looking into its eyes.

When I was growing up, dogs lived outside, chained up all day. Their purpose was to guard the house. There was no such thing as dog food — dogs ate whatever the family left over from their meals. Dogs in those days were anything but "companion animals." After my parents moved to an apartment and I left for graduate school abroad, I never had a dog. Then, five years ago, my second son started singing the "I want a dog" song. My wife disliked not just dogs but animals in general, so the idea of a pet in our home was unimaginable.

But no parent can win against their child forever. Our second son escalated his campaign to a full-blown siege, and as my wife grew exhausted from his relentless pleading, we happened to watch a Korean TV show called Dogs Are Wonderful. The show shifted our perspective: raising a dog teaches children responsibility while offering emotional bonding. Eventually, my wife and I made our son solemnly promise to walk the dog twice a day, and we adopted a puppy. That is how "Gureum" (Cloud) became a member of our family. Of course, all subsequent walks and caregiving duties fell to my wife and me. I loved Gureum from the start — the way he came scurrying to greet me when I got home from work, tail wagging furiously. Raising him alongside our two boys brought a different kind of joy entirely.

How Eye Contact Raises Oxytocin

When Gureum was young, he would sit beside me wanting to be petted but kept avoiding my eyes. "Do dogs just not make eye contact?" I thought, and did not think much of it. But as Gureum grew, he began to meet my gaze. When we locked eyes for a long time, it felt as though something was passing between us — a genuine connection.

After reading the Science paper, I began to understand the process behind this. Dr. Nagasawa brought 30 dogs and their 30 owners into a laboratory, allowed them to interact, and then measured oxytocin levels. Dr. Nagasawa had already shown in 2009 that the deeper the bond between dog and owner, the longer they maintained eye contact. This new study focused on whether oxytocin was the determining factor in that bond and eye-contact duration.

Sure enough, the longer the eye contact between dog and owner, the higher the owner's oxytocin levels climbed. And it was not just the owners — the dogs' oxytocin levels rose in tandem with their owners'. What made this study especially interesting was that when the same experiment was conducted with wolves and their handlers, oxytocin levels did not increase. More recently, researchers even demonstrated that oxytocin is involved when dogs shed tears upon reuniting with their owners after a period of separation.1

To confirm that the oxytocin increase in humans was specifically due to eye contact with dogs, the researchers artificially raised the dogs' oxytocin levels and then observed the interaction between the dogs and their owners. The results were exactly as predicted: the dogs' elevated oxytocin extended the duration of eye contact with their owners and even raised the owners' oxytocin levels. This finding is entirely consistent with earlier research showing that when fathers inhaled oxytocin, their eye-contact time with their children increased, and the children's oxytocin levels rose as well.

When we meet, communicate, and connect with others, some people leave us feeling energized and warm, while others leave us irritated, drained, and regretting we ever bothered to meet them. Could the reason we feel this way be oxytocin? It is a question worth taking seriously. When we consider MRI studies showing that brain networks governing emotion, reward, and attachment activate both when mothers see photos of their children and when they see photos of their pets, perhaps our companion animals have already become true members of the family. Nagasawa's research suggests that therapy dogs may be effective for people with autism, PTSD, depression, and anxiety disorders.2

The Gift Your Dog Gives You

One day, I gave a lecture on oxytocin to retired and senior professors at Yonsei University, and afterward we sat down for lunch together. One of the professors came up to me and said: "I think you're right, Professor. I adopted a dog that had been returned by a previous owner. At first, she couldn't make eye contact with me. But once she started sleeping in my bed, she began looking into my eyes — as if she trusted me. And she stopped barking at other dogs, too."

Of course, it is possible that the dog simply became more emotionally stable after finding a loving owner, which naturally made her calmer. But given the research showing that raising a dog's oxytocin levels increases loyalty and sociality, his observation rings true. And when I see people who cannot utter a word to strangers chatting effortlessly with other dog owners during walks, I cautiously wonder whether dog lovers might simply have higher oxytocin levels.

Everyday Oxytocin Boost: Today, try making eye contact with the dog who has been waiting for you all day. If you do not have a dog, why not consider adopting one? Of course, adoption should only happen after thorough learning and careful deliberation about what it takes to care for a companion animal.
