Chapter 26: Eat Your Way Up -- How Food Raises Oxytocin

-- How Food Raises Oxytocin

When Taste Became a Way of Life

Restaurant hopping — or matjip tambang, as Koreans call it — has become something close to a national pastime. Young couples and groups of friends build entire weekends around hunting down dishes they first saw on Instagram, pilgrimaging to some tiny shopfront on the strength of a single viral photograph. In Korea, "Have you eaten?" has long served as a greeting — less a dietary inquiry than a way of saying "I care about you." Until recently, food carried little significance beyond satiation. But a shift has taken place. Young Koreans now willingly stand in lines that stretch around the block, waiting an hour for a bowl of something they have never tasted. This is more than appetite. It is a generational hunger for novelty, for the sweet spot where luxury and affordability intersect, for the elevation of the everyday into the exceptional. Taste — that most intimate of senses — has become a kind of alchemy, transforming the ordinary into the extraordinary. Consider the humble tteokbokki and twigim — spicy rice cakes and golden-fried street fritters — sold from carts and tiny stalls in every Korean neighborhood. Once mere snacks, they have become emblems of a national palate, their addictive sweet-salty-spicy rhythm now recognized worldwide. Flavor, in Korea, has become a form of identity.

And it is no longer enough to simply eat well. You must document it. Instagram and its visual economy have turned the pre-meal photograph into a secular ritual, and the line between eating and performing has blurred. In Korea, taste has become aesthetics.

Alongside restaurant culture, there is mukbang — a phenomenon that originated in South Korea and has since conquered the world. The word, a portmanteau of meokneun (eating) and bangsong (broadcast), entered English untranslated: mukbang. These are not merely videos of people eating. They are communal experiences — of empathy, of shared pleasure, and sometimes of healing. Tzuyang, a near-mythic figure in the mukbang universe, has spoken about receiving messages from cancer patients undergoing fasting therapy who said her broadcasts brought them comfort — that watching someone else eat with such uninhibited joy was, in its way, nourishing.

And for the growing legions of honbap-reo — solo diners — mukbang offers something gentler: companionship by proxy. Watching someone eat on a screen while eating alone at your own table creates a curious intimacy, a feeling of belonging to a community you have never met. The latest evolution is ASMR mukbang, where high-fidelity microphones capture every crunch and slurp in exquisite detail. The genre's popularity speaks to a deep human need to feel accompanied at the table. So tonight, you too may find yourself watching a mukbang and reaching — almost involuntarily — for a pot to boil ramen.

Lonely and Getting Heavier?

But whether it is restaurant hopping or mukbang, a shadow lurks at the edges of all this pleasure: what it does to our waistlines. And with solo dining on the rise — more people sitting alone in restaurants, scrolling through their phones between bites — one has to wonder whether they are swallowing loneliness along with their meals. A sobering recent study confirmed exactly this: loneliness increases appetite and promotes weight gain. As if the ache of isolation were not punishment enough, it comes with a physical toll. There is something almost cruel about it.

The stakes of eating alone go beyond the caloric. Oxytocin, as we have seen, suppresses appetite and helps prevent diabetes — but eating itself can also raise oxytocin. Research has shown that consuming carbohydrates and foods like chocolate triggers the release of not only oxytocin but serotonin, making the act of eating inherently pleasurable. In evolutionary terms, this makes perfect sense. For most of human history, the problem was scarcity, not surplus. Eating voraciously whenever food appeared was a survival strategy, and a bit of a belly was a badge of prosperity. Koreans began worrying about obesity only after the economic miracle of the 1980s and '90s, when industrialization and urbanization transformed the landscape of daily life. So the drive to eat is ancient and deep. But the world has shifted beneath our feet. We are lonelier than we have ever been, and heavier, and — paradox of paradoxes — eating more while enjoying it less.1

The Oxytocin Dinner: A Shared Table

The single best food for raising oxytocin is, as I have already mentioned, fiber-rich vegetables — the kind that feed your gut microbiome. Make it a non-negotiable rule: every meal includes vegetables. Vegetables, after all, are carbohydrates — just the right kind. Unless they have been processed into something unrecognizable (canned corn, for instance), eating them raw or lightly steamed preserves their benefit. Because vitamin C is essential for the enzyme PAM (peptidylglycine alpha-amidating monooxygenase), which plays a critical role in oxytocin synthesis, citrus fruits are an excellent addition to the diet. Vitamin D, too, supports oxytocin production, and since deficiency can cascade into a host of health problems, supplementation under a physician's guidance is sensible. Better still, get outside. Sunlight drives vitamin D synthesis, so hiking, cycling, and any outdoor activity you enjoy serves double duty.2

But what you eat is only half the story. The other half is who sits across from you. A meal shared with family or close friends is a health intervention no pill can replicate. Even the humblest table — a bowl of rice still sending up its curls of steam, a pot of doenjang-jjigae bubbling away with its deep, fermented warmth — becomes a feast when shared with someone you love. That is not sentimentality; it is biochemistry. The presence of someone you care about at the table is the most potent catalyst for oxytocin release that food can offer.

Choose shared meals over solo ones whenever life allows. Eating alone exposes the body to obesity and a constellation of associated risks. Perhaps the single most beneficial dietary habit you can cultivate is this: regularly cook something yourself and invite a friend over. Set the table. Light a candle if you are feeling ambitious. Let the smell of home-cooked food fill the room. Of course, cooking should not become its own source of stress — the point is communion, not culinary performance.

The Oxytocin Way of Eating Oxytocin is intimately connected to food — not just what you eat but how. Sharing a meal raises oxytocin. And sharing does not merely mean sitting at the same table while everyone eats their own dish. It means eating from a common pot — a bubbling jeongol (Korean hot pot), a plate of pajeon (scallion pancakes) torn apart by many hands — or swapping bites from each other's bowls. Think back to your school days: desks shoved together in a circle, lunchboxes open, everyone trading side dishes — a bite of your japchae for a piece of my gyeran-mari. Your oxytocin was certainly surging. To share your food is to begin building a community. And it matters what you feed your gut. Microbial diversity is key, and among beneficial bacteria, Lactobacillus reuteri stands out for its ability to enhance oxytocin function, boosting both immunity and sociability. Many other beneficial strains improve our physical, mental, and social health through the same oxytocin pathway. These gut bacteria feed primarily on undigested plant carbohydrates — dietary fiber — and produce short-chain fatty acids that strengthen the immune system and stimulate the brain's production of BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), a molecule critical for mental health. Among vegetables, those richest in dietary fiber — chicory, Jerusalem artichoke, and onion — are especially valuable. There is an old folk claim (perhaps not entirely grounded in science) that people who eat only meat and never touch vegetables tend to be irritable and aggressive. If there is any truth in it, the explanation may lie in the gut: without fiber, the beneficial bacteria that enable oxytocin to function properly simply cannot thrive.
Oxytocin Boost: Tonight, embark on your own culinary adventure. Better yet, order something you have been craving, sit down with a friend or someone you love, and eat it together while the conversation flows. A slice of cake, a bowl of hobakjuk (sweet pumpkin porridge), that chicken-bone broth your mother used to simmer for hours — whatever feeds the soul as much as the stomach.
