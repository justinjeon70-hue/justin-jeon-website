Part Three: The Oxytocin That Makes Us Better People

Chapter 10: Oxytocin, the Hormone That Believes in You

Trust is among the most essential elements of human life. A relationship built on solid trust is the bedrock of existence and the bulwark of survival. Human civilization itself was erected on trust and belief. To live, we must trust — constantly, unavoidably, in dozens of people we will never truly know. The trust that the bank teller managing my retirement account is not secretly siphoning funds. The trust that the surgeon will deftly remove the tumor from my colon. The trust that the civil servant will not browse my personal records on a whim. The trust that the subway operator will carry me safely across the bridge over the Han River. Strip away any one of these, and I could not concentrate on my own life for a single moment.

The Latin word for trust is fides. From it came the English word faith. Both carry the meaning of binding something tightly with a cord. Consider the word fiddle — a medieval nickname for the violin — which also derives from fides. For a stringed instrument to produce its true sound, each string must be stretched taut, bound firmly enough to generate precisely the right tension. This is what we call tuning. Even a Stradivarius worth a king's ransom would be nothing more than an elegant ornament if its strings hung slack.

Oxytocin, the Tuner of Trust

Between people, too, a certain tension in the cord is necessary. This is why we call the bond of faithfulness between spouses fidelity — a word that shares the same ancient root. In Shakespeare's Othello, it was not an enemy's sword but a husband's distrust that destroyed what had been a relationship of incandescent love. The tragedy began with a single seed of suspicion, planted by the scheming lieutenant Iago. Slowly, Othello began to doubt Desdemona's faithfulness. One by one, the taut strings snapped. Blinded by jealousy, he murdered his wife. Only after she lay dead did he learn that she had been faithful all along. He was consumed by remorse, but trust once shattered cannot be reassembled, and the dead do not return. A broken string cannot simply be knotted and reattached to the instrument. It must be replaced. But a string that has merely gone slack? For that, you call a tuner. And so the old piano gathering dust in our living room is reborn as a true instrument through the skilled hands of a professional tuner — a careful tightening here, a gentle loosening there.

Have you ever watched how a colleague treats others at work? Picture a workplace filled entirely with people who are relentlessly positive, who overlook weaknesses and lavish praise on strengths, who volunteer for the hardest tasks, who take care never to inconvenience others, and who greet every mistake with a reassuring "Hey, these things happen" and a pat on the back. That workplace would be nothing short of paradise.

Paul Zak, a professor at Claremont Graduate University and one of the foremost researchers on oxytocin and human relationships, calls oxytocin the "Golden Rule hormone." He explains it this way: when someone treats me well, my brain produces oxytocin, and that oxytocin compels me to be kind in return. In my reading, this resonates more with Rousseau's social contract than with Locke's. Human society rests on mutual trust among its members: *If I help that person, surely that person will help me. And indeed, reciprocating kindness from someone who once helped you in your hour of need is simply human nature. This is the law that oxytocin teaches us.

In 2004, Professor Zak became the first scientist to demonstrate that oxytocin makes people more trusting. He divided participants into two groups — givers and receivers — and handed each giver ten dollars to start a simple trust game. When a giver sent the full ten dollars to a receiver, the receiver's account was automatically credited with triple that sum — thirty dollars. The receiver could then return whatever amount they chose to the original giver. The most mutually satisfying outcome would be for the giver to send all ten, prompting the receiver to return fifteen of the thirty, leaving each with fifteen dollars. The catch: neither player knew what the other would do. The experiment ran under two conditions. In one, the receiver was told in advance that the giver trusted them and intended to send a generous amount. In the other, nothing was said at all.1

\* The Golden Rule — "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you" — originates in the Bible. It is no exaggeration to say that Western social ethics were built upon this single precept.

What happened? Receivers who had been told the giver trusted them showed a fifty percent spike in blood oxytocin compared to the control group, and they returned fifty-three percent of the money they received. Those who were told nothing returned a mere eighteen percent. The implication is extraordinary: simply knowing that someone trusts us is enough to trigger a surge of oxytocin in the brain. Conversely, the moment we sense we are being doubted — that stinging sensation Koreans call binjeongsanghada, the feeling of wounded pride — we grow hostile and are more likely to act against the other person's interests.1

This is precisely why a child who has just decided to stop playing and start studying will suddenly lose all motivation the instant his mother barks, "Go study already!" And it is why a pitcher who trusts his fielders to catch anything that gets past the batter can throw with every ounce of his arm on the mound. But a pitcher who suspects his teammates will fumble every ball? His legs will tremble up there, no matter how many zeros are in his contract.

Oxytocin: Growing Trust from Trust

Trust begets trust. This is the magic that oxytocin works. But does inhaling oxytocin truly make a person more trusting and generous? A landmark experiment at the University of Zurich put this question to the test. Participants inhaled oxytocin and then played an investment game with simple rules. Each investor received a sum of money and was encouraged to invest it in four other players. The amount was entirely their choice. If you received 100 euros and invested nothing, the game ended immediately and the 100 euros was yours to keep. But if you invested the full amount, your recipient's account would be credited with three times the sum — 300 euros. The recipient could then return however much they wished. That meant they could pocket the entire 300, return half, or even give back every cent. What would you do? For reference, 100 euros is roughly 143,000 Korean won at current exchange rates.

What did the Zurich team find? Exactly what you might expect. Participants who inhaled oxytocin invested seventeen percent more than the control group, and the proportion who invested the maximum possible amount was twice as high. If I were an entrepreneur, I would want every investor's oxytocin to be through the roof.

But the initial experiment had a limitation: once an entrepreneur betrayed the investor's trust, the investor had no recourse and the game simply ended. So the Zurich team designed a follow-up. They asked: even after being betrayed — repeatedly — would people who inhaled oxytocin continue to trust and invest? The answer was yes. Even when returns fell short of expectations, the oxytocin group kept investing. Oxytocin, it turned out, is a truly extraordinary hormone — one that grows trust from trust. And here is what makes it even more remarkable: the very act of being kind to another person elevates our own oxytocin, and that rising oxytocin makes us kinder still. A virtuous cycle of astonishing elegance.2

These findings carry enormous implications for economics, and today many marketers are putting them into practice. The surge of interest in cognitive economics has given rise to an entirely new discipline called neuromarketing — the science of measuring physiological and neurological signals to unlock insights into customer motivation, preferences, and purchasing decisions. It is now actively deployed in creative advertising, product development, pricing strategy, and beyond, drawing on brain scans and eye-tracking technology to build systematic marketing tools. If you are a business professional preparing for a high-stakes meeting with a buyer, it may be worth thinking about how to raise that buyer's oxytocin.
