Part Two: Oxytocin for the Soul

Chapter 8: Oxytocin, the Pain Killer

"I want to face my failures on my own. If I must fail, let me fail alone — completely, purely alone. I'm an adult, Father. I don't want you barging into my life and shielding me from every setback. Please. Stop."

This was a line from the 2022 Korean drama Extraordinary Attorney Woo that stopped me mid-breath. Korean parents tend to treasure their children with an almost sacred intensity — geum-iya, ok-iya, the old expression goes: "my gold, my jade." The degree varies, but the impulse is universal. Every parent aches when their child aches, winces when their child winces. And sometimes, in the desperation to absorb their child's pain, parents try to feel it in their place.

But pain is an indispensable sensation for growth. No flower blooms without trembling in the wind. No young creature matures without the cost of growing pains. You become an adult through suffering; you grow wiser in proportion to what you have endured.

Yet a troubling new attitude toward pain has taken root. "Happiness without suffering" — an impossible dream — has become a cultural aspiration, spawning a generation of pleasure-seekers who want the reward without the cost. Parents lavish praise for fear of wounding; teachers bite back correction for fear of complaints. When discipline vanishes from schools and homes, children may suffer fewer immediate hurts, but they also lose the muscle that carries them through adversity.

Pleasure and Pain: Two Sides of a Single Coin

Anna Lembke, author of Dopamine Nation, argues that by overprotecting children from hardship, we have made them afraid of even minor difficulties. By inflating their self-esteem with dishonest praise and concealed sacrifice, we have created a new hedonistic era in which young people demand comfort as a right, tolerate frustration as an outrage, and remain blind to their own flaws. Unable to sit with discomfort, this generation reaches, with increasing speed, for whatever will change their mood fastest — binge-watching, painkillers, the full pharmacopoeia of addictive substances.

The numbers in the United States are staggering. Since the year 2000, more than one million Americans have died from the misuse of opioid painkillers — a death toll exceeding that of gun violence. And here is a fact so remarkable it deserves its own silence: the brain regions that process pain and the brain regions that process pleasure are one and the same. Pleasure and pain are not opposites separated by a wall. They are neighbors sharing a single room, divided only by a paper-thin curtain. When we chase pleasure relentlessly to escape suffering, or lean on opioids to erase every ache, we end up destroying even our capacity for ordinary, unspectacular, everyday happiness.

People are growing lonelier. The COVID-19 pandemic, which turned social isolation into a public health directive, only deepened the trend. Loneliness causes pain. Pain makes us withdraw. Withdrawal deepens isolation. The spiral tightens with each turn. A recent Australian study of 12,517 respondents — 927 of whom suffered from chronic pain — examined the interplay between social connection, pain, and physical activity. Participants were asked: "When I need help, can I find someone to help me?" and "Do I have many friends?" The results revealed that greater social support was associated with greater physical activity — and that pain was the mediating variable. In plainer terms: the more friends you have, the less pain you feel; and because you feel less pain, you move more.1

It is already well established that doing things together raises the pain threshold.* Consider rowing — arguably the most punishing of endurance sports. It hurts considerably less when performed with teammates than alone. People who exercise with friends or colleagues can tolerate twice as much pain afterward. We feel less pain singing in a chorus than singing solo. We laugh harder and hurt less watching a comedy with someone beside us than watching it by ourselves. The old saying proves truer than poetry: a burden shared is a burden halved.2

\*Pain threshold: the level of stimulus at which pain first registers. A threshold of 5 means an intensity of 4 goes unfelt; a threshold of 3 means that same intensity of 4 is persistent — and potentially chronic.

Oxytocin Is a Natural Painkiller

Can behaviors that raise oxytocin actually relieve pain? Earlier, I described how oxytocin affects mothers during childbirth. It acts on the brain and spinal cord to dull the perception of pain while simultaneously helping us forget the pain we have already experienced. In animal studies, twenty-nine out of thirty-three experiments confirmed that oxytocin raises the pain threshold.3

Human studies tell a similar story. A particularly illuminating experiment was conducted in Germany in 2020. Eighty couples who had been living together for at least a year were divided into four groups. The first inhaled oxytocin only. The second inhaled a placebo while also learning and practicing a positive communication technique twice daily. The third inhaled real oxytocin and practiced positive communication. The fourth did neither. Researchers then measured wound healing and pain levels across all four groups.

The results were striking — and gendered. For men, inhaled oxytocin was more effective at reducing pain. For women, positive communication with their partner was more effective.

This is not entirely surprising. Women already circulate four to five times more oxytocin than men, so supplemental inhalation delivers a proportionally smaller boost. But the finding that women experienced less pain simply by having warm, constructive conversations with their partners is deeply compelling. In another study, researchers administered oxytocin and then deliberately induced pain with needle pricks and laser pulses. The oxytocin significantly reduced what participants reported feeling. Within the scientific community, oxytocin's analgesic properties are now considered well established; current research is focused on mapping the precise mechanisms by which this molecule dampens pain signals.4

A word for those living with the diagnosis of "chronic pain syndrome" — the label doctors apply when every test comes back normal and the cause remains unknown. The name sounds clinical, but it is essentially medicine's admission of defeat. These patients are typically referred to neurology, on the assumption that if nothing is wrong with the body, the answer must lie in the mind. If someone you love — a child, a spouse, a parent — suffers from chronic pain syndrome, please understand that dismissing them as lazy, weak, or dishonest will only deepen their suffering. Sometimes a warm word is stronger than a prescription. Sometimes holding someone's hand, rubbing their shoulders, driving to a town they have never visited and sharing a meal at a restaurant with a view — these are worth more than most pills on the pharmacy shelf.

My ninety-one-year-old mother sometimes calls to say her head hurts. I rush over and massage her shoulders and neck. She smiles almost immediately and declares herself cured. My sisters laugh and say she was faking it — that she just missed her son. But I wonder: perhaps her pain genuinely improved because her son arrived, sat down beside her, held her hands, kneaded the tension from her neck — and in doing so, coaxed a surge of oxytocin into her bloodstream.

If you know someone suffering from chronic pain, encourage them — gently, persistently — to go out and meet people, even when it hurts. See friends. Share a good meal. Watch a movie and laugh until your sides ache. Go for a walk together, however short. These may be the most effective painkillers we possess. If it is true that humans are social creatures to the marrow, then genuine happiness comes only when we connect — when we trade emotions, share silences, and allow ourselves to be seen. Yes, relationships bring pain too. But if we seal ourselves away to avoid the hurt that comes with human closeness, we may find ourselves drowning in a far greater pain: the pain of no one at all. Let us summon the courage to cherish the people already near us a little more fiercely, and to approach the people we do not yet know a little more openly. Oxytocin will meet you on that path. It always does.
