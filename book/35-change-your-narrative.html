Part Eight: The Power of Narrative

Chapter 35: Change Your Narrative, Change Your Life

, Change Your Life

A narrative is a story — but not just any story. It is the story of how a life has been lived.

Of course, most narratives we encounter in daily life lean toward fiction. Films do this. Theater does this. Paintings, and sometimes music, do this too. I am no connoisseur of the arts, but not long ago my graduate students dragged me to an exhibition of Lee Jung-seob, one of Korea's most revered painters — the man famous above all for his sinewy, anguished paintings of bulls. I walked in brimming with expectation. What I found were tiny, palm-sized sketches scrawled on postcards, and I caught myself thinking: I could draw something like that.

Then came the paintings he made on Jeju Island while living with his wife, and something shifted. When I saw the drawings of children scratched onto cigarette-foil wrappers, a quiet tremor passed through my chest. I pulled out my phone to look up Lee Jung-seob's story, and in the moment I began to grasp the meaning behind these fragile little images, that small tremor became a tidal wave that struck something deep inside me.

Lee Jung-seob was born into a wealthy family during the Japanese occupation of Korea. He showed early talent for drawing, traveled to Japan to study, and there fell headlong in love with a Japanese woman. He brought her back to a Korea seething with contempt for the Japanese and took a job as a schoolteacher, navigating the razor-thin space between neighbors who resented his wife and a wife who felt their hostility — channeling the tension into his art. When the North turned communist, he was branded a bourgeois and hounded for it. Then the Korean War erupted, and the family fled across live fire and shifting front lines to the very bottom of the peninsula — Jeju Island. There was a brief, improbable stretch of happiness: with nothing to eat, they caught crabs on the shore, and the whole family was, for a moment, content. But poverty is patient. Unable to outrun it, Lee Jung-seob sent his wife and children to Japan — her homeland — swearing he would follow soon after. Nearly every day he drew pictures and wrote letters on postcards to the wife he loved and the children who were the light of his eyes, soothing his loneliness by sketching the reunion he dreamed of. His life, though, only tangled further. He drank. His health collapsed. And this man who had spent his final years pouring all his longing into tears and pigment simply slipped away — just like that.

As I read his letters and encountered his narrative, something cracked open inside me. Suddenly his paintings looked like entirely different works. The crabs his family had to catch and eat to survive — Lee Jung-seob felt wretched about those crabs, and he painted them as an apology, begging their forgiveness through art. When I saw the painting of children fishing, and the one of the whole family holding hands in a wide circle with radiant, unclouded smiles, I could no longer hold myself together. I found a corner and sat down, hoping my students would not notice, trying to compose myself. It was no use. Grief flooded through me and I wept openly — shamelessly, uncontrollably. Before the weight this man had carried as a father and provider, before the endurance of a human being who bore pain and heartbreak with his whole body in an era when nothing at all was certain, I could only bow my head.

And though my situation was incomparable to his, something about his story called up my own — the year I spent as a gireugi appa, a "wild-goose father," the Korean term for men who live apart from families sent abroad for the children's education. I had left my family in Canada. I had enough of everything, materially. And yet everything felt insufficient. That bone-deep loneliness, that solitude that pierces you to the marrow — it was all there again. The narrative of life embedded in Lee Jung-seob's paintings went beyond something I understood. I felt it with my entire body.

The Experiencing Self and the Remembering Self

It was the power of narrative. The experience shook me so profoundly that I brought my wife and son back for a second visit. Lee Jung-seob moved me again — differently this time — and I found myself seeking out every painting of his I could find. The experience reminded me of Daniel Kahneman, the Nobel Prize-winning founder of behavioral economics. Kahneman famously divided the human psyche into the experiencing self and the remembering self, confessing that happiness researchers — himself included — had been producing flawed work for years because they had failed to distinguish between the two. The experiencing self is the self that feels in the moment: savoring a delicious meal, feeling happy on a date, feeling the wind-in-your-face thrill of hang-gliding with your partner on vacation.

But suppose your partner cheated on you and you divorced. Afterward you learned that during that very vacation — during the hang-gliding and the laughter — she was already involved with another man. The trip is no longer a happy memory. Now consider the opposite: a man who suffered so terribly during his military service that he contemplated suicide. Yet decades after discharge, he brings up his army days at every gathering. Why? Because he found meaning in the suffering. This self — the one that looks back at past experience and weaves it into narrative — is the remembering self.1

Kahneman gave the remembering self another name: the storyteller. More recently, Professor Kim Joo-hwan, whose book Inner Communication has generated enormous resonance in both public and academic circles, takes the idea further with what he calls the background self — the deeper layer that interprets the events of our complex lives, assigns them meaning, and stores them as remembered episodes. I agree entirely. But here is the crucial addition: we do not live alone. Our narratives are not solitary monologues. Every great film has a villain alongside the hero; fortune alternates with disaster. Consider the narrative of Professor Lee Ji-sun, who overcame full-body burns to become a professor at her alma mater. She describes her experience with quiet grace: "I met with an accident — and then I successfully parted ways with it." Those words have moved countless people. She was riding in a car with her brother when a drunk driver struck them. The car exploded and her entire body was burned. She survived — perhaps because her remembering self was made of something that would not break.2

The reason I could feel her story so deeply in my bones is that I, too, was severely burned in an accident during my freshman year of college and stood at the edge of death. Even after my body healed, freeing myself psychologically from the scars took years. This is what narrative does: someone else's story meets your own and together they compose something new. And those new narratives become real — I first met Professor Lee Ji-sun in person in 2003, and twenty years later, we still share a relationship rich with meaning.

The Story That Changes Your Life

Narrative changes lives. I was born the youngest child and only son among six siblings — one boy and five older sisters. As a child I was crippled by an inferiority complex about my appearance and drifted through school like a satellite that never locked into orbit. My grades hovered reliably in the lower-middle range. The one arena where I held my own was athletics and fighting — until a classmate's fist connected squarely with my jaw, shattered the bone, and sent me to the hospital. The humiliation was total. I could not even face the boy's mother, who appeared every day with homemade bone broth, begging me to forgive her son. The thought of returning to school and confronting the reality — the loser who got his jaw broken in a fight — filled me with dread. The night before my discharge, I wished the world would simply end.

One root of my childhood shame was my parents' education. Born in rural 1930s Korea, they had almost no formal schooling. My father finished elementary school but could not continue — the family could not afford it — so he studied Chinese classics at the village seodang instead. And yet he trusted me, unconditionally. Even when I was a high schooler slipping in and out of nightclubs, he handed me pocket money and asked only that I not come home too late.

My academic failures, it turned out, were a gift. Because I struggled for years as a poor student, I understand viscerally what it feels like when understanding refuses to come. That is why I excel at reaching students who are falling behind. The result: I received Yonsei University's Outstanding Faculty Award for Teaching — given to roughly seven professors a year — on three separate occasions, and then became only the second person ever to receive the Supreme Teaching Excellence Award, which recognizes those who have earned the base award three times. The ADHD that once tormented me became a superpower, enabling relentless multitasking. My chronic restlessness — the inability to stay interested in any one thing for long — drove me across a remarkably wide intellectual landscape: adaptive sports, exercise psychology, exercise physiology, exercise medicine, and artificial intelligence.

This breadth is why I hold concurrent professorships at Yonsei University not only in my home Department of Sport and Leisure Studies but also at the Cancer Prevention Center of the College of Medicine and the Graduate School of Artificial Intelligence — a rare arrangement even at Yonsei. Long before "interdisciplinary research" became a fashionable phrase, I was already collaborating across departments of kinesiology, rehabilitation medicine, medicine, and engineering during my Canadian graduate school years. Looking back, every single one of my shortcomings became a strength, and because of them — all of them — I became the person I am today. I sometimes wonder: what would have happened if my parents had been highly educated? They probably would have hovered over my schoolwork, berated me for my grades, and nagged me endlessly. Given how fiercely I resist being nagged, I might well have gone off the rails entirely.

Recently my eldest son — who has been caring for his younger brother, a high school senior, back in Canada — came to Korea bearing two gifts. The first was introducing me to the work of Robert Sapolsky and Daniel Kahneman. The second was a copy of Viktor Frankl's Man's Search for Meaning. In that book there is a parable about a chess master. Someone asks: "Master, what is the single most important move in chess?" A foolish question. Chess is a game of reading your opponent's moves and responding with brilliance.

Our lives work the same way. "What is the most meaningful thing in your life?" The answer must differ from person to person and from moment to moment — and it should. Every human being walking this earth must carry a reason for being here. If that reason has not announced itself, you must go looking for it. That is why Frankl's original title is Man's Search for Meaning. Through meaning, Frankl healed his wounds, a process he called logotherapy. And when meaning is found, a new narrative begins. You must reinterpret your past. You must revisit today's failure from the vantage point of tomorrow. Only then can you write a new narrative aimed at your future.3

I believe — firmly — that oxytocin can help us write those narratives. I am not suggesting you inhale oxytocin through your nose. I am talking about the lifestyle that raises it. Meet more people. Share meals. Have long conversations. Act more selflessly. Volunteer. Pay attention to the people around you. Share more physical affection. Express more gratitude. Exercise together. Laugh together until tears stream down your faces. Watch great films together. Seek out wonderful restaurants. Listen to powerful stories whenever you can. And the most important thing of all? Create your own narrative. Because our lives — yesterday, today, tomorrow — are nothing more and nothing less than bundles of events waiting for someone to give them meaning. When my narrative meets yours and becomes ours, we gain access to a happier, more meaningful life.

Now it is your turn to write your oxytocin story. Your narrative.

Oxytocin in Action: Why not draft your own narrative right now? Just as television dramas organize storylines into episodes, decide how many episodes your life narrative will have, and give each one a title.
