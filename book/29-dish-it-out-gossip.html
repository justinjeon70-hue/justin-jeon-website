Chapter 29: Dish It Out, and It Goes Up -- Gossip vs. Backstabbing

, and It Goes Up — Gossip vs. Backstabbing

Nothing in the world is more entertaining than a good gossip session. On this point, there can be no disagreement. Office workers huddle together to critique the fashion choices of their bald-headed department head. Students gather to imitate their professor's lecturing quirks. On any given morning, neighborhood women congregate at a local cafe to commiserate about their husbands, while men meet over drinks to roast that one friend who was a big shot in high school but has amounted to nothing since. "Me? No, I never gossip. Absolutely not," insists the person who, the moment a politician's name comes up, rolls up their sleeves and dives headfirst into roasting the president.

Even the most refined person, after a round of drinks, will methodically place each acquaintance upon the chopping block and slice them apart with surgical precision of the tongue. They will even summon the wispy comb-over hairstyle of a former U.S. president or the peculiar lip-pursing pronunciation habits of another. All of this counts as gossip? Indeed, the vast majority of what we discuss with friends, colleagues, and casual acquaintances is gossip. Believe it or not, gossip has colonized most of our everyday language.

And for good reason — gossip is one of the quintessentially human social behaviors that makes us most human.1

The Surprising Power of Gossip to Calm the Brain

You might think we do not engage in deep thought while gossiping, but in fact, the human mind is spinning at full speed the entire time. You nod as though listening intently, making eye contact with the other person, but inside your head, thoughts are swirling: "So you're telling me I should agree that this person is terrible? But he's really not that bad." Or: "That guy is the worst — so you think he's the worst too!" You think, "Fine, whatever you say," and then immediately counter with, "But do we really need to paint him as such a villain?" In the moment before you decide whether to respond, you have mentally placed and removed several people from the guillotine. In this way, even as we talk about others, we perform an enormous amount of brain activity. And through this process, gossip becomes a vital ritual that distinguishes friend from foe and determines solidarity and bonds.

In a sense, what gossip is to humans, grooming may be to primates. Primates spend about 20 percent of their daily waking hours picking through the fur and skin of fellow primates. They straighten each other's fur, gently stroke one another. This is called grooming, and through it, primates confirm their social bonds. During grooming, an extraordinary cascade of chemical reactions occurs inside the primate body. Endorphins are released. Dopamine goes up. Cortisol goes down. These neurochemical shifts lower the primate's daily stress levels and maintain emotional equilibrium. Heart rate drops. Tension dissolves. Through grooming, primates experience empathy and intimacy. The fact that the grooming time of gorillas so closely mirrors the gossip time of humans is deeply significant.

Yuval Noah Harari, in Sapiens, identified gossip as the most critical element of the cognitive revolution. "Gossip is a malicious ability, but cooperation among large numbers requires gossip. The ability that Homo sapiens acquired 70,000 years ago enabled them to chat for hours on end." According to Harari, primates can only groom one-on-one, but humans can effectively gossip in groups of up to four or five. On this topic, Professor Robin Dunbar, who studied the relationship between primate brain size and group size, found that the larger the social group a primate belongs to, the larger its neocortex — the brain region associated with concentration, contextual interpretation, and language ability. Perhaps we have become smarter precisely by meeting other people and gossiping endlessly.2

The Correlation Between Gossip and Oxytocin

Just as grooming reduces cortisol in primates, does gossip do the same in humans? Professor Konrad Rudnicki of Belgium gathered 66 participants, asked them to gossip, and then measured their cortisol and endorphin levels, as well as sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system activation. The results showed that while gossip did not increase endorphins, cortisol decreased noticeably. Moreover, people who had a positive attitude toward gossip showed an even greater cortisol reduction and significantly higher parasympathetic activity.

An even more fascinating study came from Professor Natascia Brondino's research team in Italy that same year. This study was conducted exclusively with female university students. In the first condition, they had emotionally supportive conversations that were not gossip. Cortisol levels decreased significantly. Then they had the participants gossip. This time, not only did cortisol decrease, but oxytocin increased significantly as well.3

When I read this far in the paper, I slapped my knee. "Ah, so to raise oxytocin, you need to join in the gossip!" Looking back, whenever my wife would gossip about a coworker, I had the baffling habit of defending someone I had never even met — and getting scolded for it. "Whose side are you on?" She was right. Why on earth was I defending a complete stranger? There is a Korean saying that "a husband is always on the other side," and indeed, men sometimes stir up trouble where none was asked for. When your wife starts dishing the gossip, here is how you should respond. This is the formula. Male readers, memorize it: "Is that person insane? Why would anyone treat an innocent person like that? Should I go say something to them?" And while you say this, do not forget to hold your wife's hand tightly. Sparks will fly between your locked gazes, and the oxytocin levels of both husband and wife will soar.

The truth is, when we speak positively about other people, we feel a sense of moral satisfaction, but it does not necessarily relieve stress or deepen our bond with the person we are talking to. On the other hand, the moment we start talking negatively about someone else, we suddenly become co-conspirators. "Alright, we're in the same boat now, got it?" Call it a sense of complicity, or call it camaraderie. That is why we begin gossip sessions with phrases like: "I'm only telling you this!" or "Don't tell anyone you heard this from me!" or "You absolutely cannot tell another soul, okay?" Of course, there is always someone around who immediately passes the story along. So while trying to raise your oxytocin through gossip, be careful — you might end up tangling all the relationships around you, sending your cortisol through the roof instead.
