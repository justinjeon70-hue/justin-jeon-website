Part Three: The Oxytocin That Makes Us Better People

Chapter 11: Oxytocin, the Altruistic Hormone

O. Henry's short story "The Gift of the Magi" is, at its heart, a parable about selflessness. James and Della were desperately poor, yet they loved each other more fiercely than anyone. Christmas was approaching and they had nothing — no savings, no plan. Each wanted desperately to give the other an unforgettable gift, but neither could scrape together the money. In the end, James sold his most treasured possession — a gold pocket watch handed down through generations like a family scripture — to buy Della a set of ornamental combs. Della, meanwhile, cut and sold her long, cascading golden hair — hair so magnificent it would have made a fairy-tale princess weep with envy — to buy James a platinum watch chain. Each sacrificed what was most precious to them for the sake of the other. And by fate's cruel irony, each gift rendered the other useless: combs for hair that was gone, a chain for a watch that had been sold.

When James saw the tangled irony of their exchange, he said to his weeping wife: "Darling, let us put our presents away for now. They are too precious to use just yet." Note the double meaning of that word — present as gift and present as this very moment. O. Henry reportedly said of his characters: "I have told the tale of two foolish young people who sacrificed, for each other, the greatest treasures of their home. But let me speak a last word to the wise: of all who give gifts, these two were the wisest. Of all who give and receive gifts, such as they are the wisest. Everywhere, they are the wisest. They are the magi."

How Altruistic Are We?

How altruistic can a human being truly be? The willingness to surrender part of what you possess so that another person may flourish — this is what we call altruism. What would happen if husbands and wives, teachers and students, employers and workers all treated one another with genuine selflessness? How many of us could say, as Moses did in the Bible, "Blot my name from your book" if it meant saving a people from destruction? How many could do what Captain Robert F. Scott did during his doomed Antarctic expedition — urging his companions, "I am already finished; consume my body and survive this storm"?

There is a famous thought experiment that illuminates the tension at the heart of altruism: the prisoner's dilemma, from which game theory was born. The setup is now so well known it has appeared on television shows worldwide — including Running Man, the beloved Korean variety program, where similar games are a staple. In the classic scenario, two accomplices are arrested, but the police lack hard evidence and need confessions. The suspects are placed in separate cells, and each is offered a deal: confess, and your sentence drops to two years. But there is a catch — if you confess and your partner stays silent, you may bear the full weight of the crime alone. Of course, your partner, sitting in the cell next door, is wrestling with the same calculus. Sound familiar? This is the dilemma that gave birth to game theory, articulated by John Nash — the mathematician immortalized in A Beautiful Mind. Through iterated rounds, participants tend to gravitate not toward the best possible outcome but toward avoiding the worst, arriving at what is known as a Nash equilibrium.

In 2010, the psychologist Carsten De Dreu of the University of Amsterdam published three elegant experiments in Science, each grounded in game theory. The first asked: does oxytocin promote altruism toward members of one's own group? The second: does this altruistic impulse appear only in people who are naturally cooperative, or does it also emerge in those who tend toward selfishness? The third: when aggression is framed as self-defense, does oxytocin fuel that too?

The results were striking. When participants inhaled oxytocin, they became significantly more willing to share resources with members of their own group — and this held true regardless of whether they were naturally cooperative or not. But oxytocin did not increase parochial altruism — the kind of aggression directed at outsiders in the name of protecting one's own. The distinction matters enormously. The essence of true altruism is self-sacrifice for others. Taken to an extreme, the most "altruistic" people in history might be terrorists — individuals who kill citizens of other nations in the name of their homeland. Mercifully, oxytocin promotes only the pure, selfless variety of altruism, not the narrow and tribal kind.1

Elevate Your Altruistic Happiness with Oxytocin

When do we feel more alive — acting selfishly or acting generously? You hardly need a research paper to know the answer. When I give someone I love what they need and watch their face light up, my own happiness swells in proportion. This is simply how we are built. When a child's birthday approaches, no parent grumbles about "wasting money" on a party at a family restaurant. Watching your child devour a meal with pure, unselfconscious joy is its own reward. As the Korean saying goes, "A parent feels full just watching food go into their child's mouth."

For me, the happiest moments as a professor come when I secure a research grant large enough to offer scholarships to my students. A close second is hearing a student's voice on the phone, brimming with excitement, telling me their paper has been accepted for publication as first author. Altruistic acts push us toward the most intense forms of joy. The Korean singer and philanthropist Sean — widely admired for his tireless giving — once described the happiness he feels from helping others unconditionally as being "like a drug." That drug, it turns out, is oxytocin.

Money cannot buy happiness. Beyond what sustains the basics of life, wealth often marks not the beginning of joy but the onset of misery. Ironically, the more people fixate on money, the unhappier they become; the wealthier they grow, the more time they spend alone rather than giving. Money, it seems, pushes people away.

Elizabeth Dunn, a professor at the University of British Columbia, and Michael Norton of Harvard Business School, meticulously analyzed the happiness, annual income, and spending habits of 632 people. They paid special attention to spending categories: how much went to basic necessities versus prosocial spending — money spent on socializing, shared experiences, and doing things for others. The amount people spent on themselves bore no relationship whatsoever to their happiness. But prosocial spending was powerfully correlated with well-being.

These findings were later published as the book Happy Money. To push the hypothesis further, Dunn and Norton tracked employees who received bonuses of roughly 5,000 dollars, examining how they spent the money before and six to eight weeks afterward. The results were identical. The more people had spent on others, the happier they were. We experience genuine fulfillment when we share what we have, or when we see someone else light up because of our generosity. Humans are simply wired this way.2

Does oxytocin play a role in this instinct to give? Paul Zak's research team divided ninety-six students into three groups. The first group received professional massages while playing a trust game. The second received only massages. The third played the trust game with no massage. Students in the first two groups enjoyed fifteen minutes of skilled bodywork. The results were revealing: massage alone did not meaningfully elevate oxytocin levels. But the combination of massage and the trust game did — oxytocin secretion was activated in that first group, and the levels tracked precisely with how much participants trusted their partners and invested.

After the experiment concluded, researchers collected voluntary donations. Students who had experienced both massage and the trust game gave an average of $17.36. Those who received only a massage gave $6.85. Those who played the trust game alone gave just $2.00. The group that received both trust and physical touch donated nearly three times as much as the massage-only group. As oxytocin levels climbed, the impulse to give grew stronger — an effect that was particularly pronounced in women. The conclusion is inescapable: the higher our oxytocin, the more we trust, the more generously we give, and the happier we become. This is the altruistic happiness that oxytocin teaches us.3

Oxytocin Booster: Examine your spending habits. How much goes toward shared experiences with others, and how much toward charitable giving? If these figures are low, consider redirecting some of your budget toward experiences enjoyed together with friends and neighbors.
