Introduction

Introduction: If You Want to Be Happy and Healthy, Oxytocin

Every evening, the news delivers another blow. A young man who spent years failing to find work, who retreated to his room and never came out, until one day he gave up on life altogether. An elderly mother who waited in a tiny rented room for children who never visited — her body discovered only months after she died alone. A child surviving on triangular rice balls from the convenience store who starved to death from malnutrition. A middle-aged man so isolated by his inability to connect that he, too, died in solitude. A sick mother who buried her disabled daughter and could not bring herself to follow. Each story lands like a stone on the chest.

But the world is not made only of grief.

There is the daughter who inherited a legacy of grace. For five years, her mother had run a cheonwon-bapjip — a restaurant where a full meal cost just one thousand won, less than a dollar — feeding anyone who could not afford to eat, until cancer took her life. The daughter now runs the restaurant every morning and sells insurance every afternoon, a quiet superhero in an apron. There is the man who drove his own car into floodwaters and risked his life to pull strangers from the current. The neighbor who left a basket of snacks by the front door for a taxi driver too rushed to stop for water. The mothers who lie awake praying for children who have moved to distant cities. The sons and daughters who set aside their own lives to care for a parent lost to dementia. The couples who will never make the news but who love each other with a steady, fierce tenderness. The lovers who have nothing but each other, and find that it is enough. In every corner of this world, stories of warmth are waiting to be noticed.

Oxytocin is the hormone that warms the human heart. It is the molecule that makes us capable of deeper trust and greater love. Put that way, it might sound like the stuff of greeting cards, not science. But consider this: since the first oxytocin-related paper appeared in 1929, researchers have published an astonishing 33,423 studies on this single molecule — through December 2023 alone. Shall we take a closer look?

## Oxytocin, the Love Hormone

Have you ever heard the word oxytocin? Some readers will nod; others will draw a complete blank. If you have ever undergone an induced labor, you almost certainly received an oxytocin injection. Here is what happens: oxytocin causes the uterus to contract, and each contraction signals the pituitary gland to release still more oxytocin. As levels climb, the contractions intensify. Through this self-amplifying loop, a mother's oxytocin during delivery can surge to twenty or thirty times its normal concentration. That flood of oxytocin kindles a fierce maternal love — the overwhelming urge to hold the baby, to bring the infant to the breast. When a mother's milk lets down and she instinctively guides her newborn to nurse, that is oxytocin at work. The hormone literally releases milk from the nipple. It drives a mother to care for her child with a love that feels as involuntary as breathing.

This is not merely poetic intuition. It has been demonstrated in the laboratory. Researchers found that mother rats deprived of oxytocin abandoned their pups entirely. And when virgin rats — females who had never given birth — were administered oxytocin, they began nurturing pups placed before them as tenderly as if the babies were their own.

Oxytocin is also intimately entwined with romance. Couples in new relationships show higher blood oxytocin levels than single individuals. Those whose oxytocin surged highest in the early days of courtship were far more likely to still be together six months later; those whose levels stayed low were far more likely to have split. Early oxytocin, it turns out, is one of the most reliable predictors of whether a relationship will endure. Given that emotional intimacy and physical touch between partners raise oxytocin, it is hardly surprising that couples who start with high levels tend to stay the course. Oxytocin is also released during sex — which explains the sudden, almost irrational spike in attachment that partners feel after sharing an orgasm.

## It Sharpens Social Intelligence

Oxytocin does not stop at love. In recent years, a wave of research has uncovered effects that no one previously imagined. Among the most remarkable: oxytocin profoundly shapes a child's social development. When children on the autism spectrum inhaled an oxytocin nasal spray, something close to a miracle occurred — they began reading emotions from other people's faces. The study that electrified the global scientific community was rigorously double-blind: children with autism spectrum disorder who inhaled real oxytocin detected eye movements and facial expressions far more accurately than those who received a placebo. Subsequent experiments with neurotypical adolescents yielded similar results, and the finding is now considered established science. In plain terms, oxytocin has been recognized as far more than a love hormone. It is a relationship hormone, a social-intelligence hormone.

Are some people naturally low in oxytocin? Yes. Oxytocin is closely linked to a gene called GTF2I, sometimes known as the "sociability gene." Individuals who carry a variation in the oxytocin receptor gene rs53576 have been shown to exhibit lower empathy. Think about it — we all know someone like this. Picture the office dinner: the younger staff offer a polite "Boss, stay a little longer!" hoping he will take the hint and leave. Instead, he settles back into his chair with a satisfied grin — "Oh, well, if you insist!" — picks up his glass, and launches into a solemn "Back in my day..." monologue. Utterly oblivious. Everyone at that table has learned the same truth early in their careers: what employees actually want from their boss at a company dinner is not his company — it is his corporate card. The socially intelligent move is to toss the card on the table and disappear. And as it happens, oxytocin is precisely the hormone that can dial someone's social radar up to its highest setting.

Consider this experiment. Researchers had subjects inhale oxytocin and then play an investment game. The oxytocin group trusted their partners significantly more and increased their investment amounts by 27 percent. To rule out a general increase in risk appetite, the team ran additional tests — and found no difference whatsoever in risk-taking between the oxytocin and placebo groups. This landmark study was published in Nature in 2005 and has since been cited more than five thousand times.

Here is another gem. Researchers recruited 49 couples who had been living together for at least a year, identified the topics most likely to ignite an argument between them, and then deliberately set them loose on each other. (Yes, some experiments really are that devious.) The ensuing disputes were videotaped and analyzed. In both men and women, those who had inhaled oxytocin argued in a strikingly more constructive manner — more empathetic, more positive, more inclined toward nonviolent communication. The researchers had engineered a fight, but the oxytocin couples simply could not oblige.

## Is Oxytocin a Morality Hormone?

There is even research on fidelity. Prairie voles are famously monogamous — male and female devote themselves exclusively to each other. Montane voles, by contrast, are promiscuous. Not all rodents, it seems, are created equal. The prairie vole father grooms his pups, licking their fur smooth, and shares actively in child-rearing. The montane vole father ignores his offspring entirely. But when scientists modified the montane voles' oxytocin receptors so that the hormone could function normally, those formerly indifferent fathers began caring for their pups almost overnight. This line of research is so significant that we will return to it in far greater detail later.

The magic of oxytocin has been confirmed in humans as well. In one study, married men inhaled oxytocin and then an attractive young woman approached them, flirting openly. The men were asked at what distance the woman's approach began to feel uncomfortable. Those who had inhaled oxytocin reported discomfort when the woman was about 71 centimeters away. Those on the placebo did not feel uneasy until she was just 56 centimeters away — meaning the placebo group allowed her significantly deeper into their personal space. The truly telling detail? This protective effect appeared only in married men. Single men, oxytocin or not, were perfectly content to have an attractive woman draw close. Exactly as you would expect.

## The Skin-to-Skin Hormone

And we are only getting started. Oxytocin also exerts powerful effects on the body. Recent studies have linked it to cancer, cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes, and even the pace of aging itself. Higher oxytocin levels are associated with reduced risk of cancer, heart disease, obesity, and diabetes. The hormone appears to slow aging, accelerate nerve-cell generation, strengthen bones against osteoporosis, and speed the healing of damaged muscle and wounds. Some research even suggests that elevating blood oxytocin can improve the symptoms of dementia. At this point, the more impatient among you may be asking: "Just tell me — where can I buy the spray?"

You do not need to buy anything. There is a way to raise your oxytocin dramatically without spraying a single drop up your nose.

It is touch.

When you were small and your stomach hurt, you ran to your mother without a second thought. She would lay her hands on your belly, murmuring the Korean incantation every child knows — "Umma son-eun yak-son" ("Mommy's hands are healing hands") — and somehow, impossibly, the pain would fade. That tender contact triggers the release of oxytocin. As blood levels rise, the body's pain threshold rises with them. The human body is wonderfully discriminating: it does not relay every sensation to the brain. Below a certain intensity, pain signals are simply filtered out at the level of the peripheral nerves. Studies have shown that the more skin-to-skin contact parents provide a newborn, the higher the oxytocin levels in the baby's brain — and the stronger the foundation for emotional health and cognitive development.

Korean mothers of earlier generations carried their babies against their bodies almost constantly. Even while working, they strapped their infants to their backs in a podaegi, the traditional Korean baby carrier. Western parenting, by contrast, long emphasized independence — separate nurseries, babies sleeping alone from the earliest age. But in recent years, this Korean tradition of constant physical closeness has attracted worldwide attention. The podaegi has gone global. In England, there are shops that rent them out — I looked it up, and a month's rental costs about ten British pounds. You can sense the determination of Western mothers to reclaim what Korean mothers never lost.

Sharing a meal is another route to oxytocin. This was demonstrated in a study of chimpanzees. When chimps groomed each other, their oxytocin rose modestly. But when they sat down and shared food, their urine revealed a surge. So if you are trying to win over a client or strengthen a professional relationship, perhaps skip the lengthy pitch. Sit down and eat together instead. It may be the most effective way in existence to raise the oxytocin of the person across the table. If that person is your partner, hold each other close, savor some lingering physical affection, and share something delicious. If the relationship is professional, offer a handshake warmer than usual, find a place worth remembering, and save the business talk for after the last course. And the next time you say to a friend, "We should get together for a meal sometime" — stop saying it and do it. Make time. Sit down. Talk and laugh over real food.

## The Gossip Hormone

Gossip, it turns out, is yet another oxytocin elevator. You might assume the hormone spikes during life's great achievements — getting into college, landing a dream job, earning a windfall. It does not. Those triumphs light up serotonin and dopamine. Oxytocin does not budge. What sends oxytocin soaring, remarkably, is huddling with others to gossip. When colleagues gather in a corner to gleefully dissect their insufferable manager, their tyrannical director, or an impossible client, oxytocin is having the time of its life.

There is real science behind this. Researchers assigned one group to share emotionally empathetic stories and another to gossip. In the empathy group, cortisol dropped, but oxytocin held steady. In the gossip group, cortisol dropped and oxytocin climbed.

## The Eye-Contact Hormone

One more simple way to boost oxytocin: keep a dog. When you stroke and hold your dog, oxytocin surges in both of you. Pet your dog while looking into its eyes — that combination of touch and gaze is one of the most potent oxytocin triggers we know. This is not my personal opinion. It was published in Science in 2015. Researchers have even administered oxytocin directly to dogs. With elevated levels, the dogs became more obedient, more sociable, and gazed at their owners more often. And here is the beautiful part: when the dog's oxytocin rose, the owner's rose in tandem. A feedback loop of mutual affection, written in chemistry.

In recent years, as nuclear families, single-person households, and solo meals have become the norm in South Korea, the nation's collective oxytocin has been quietly falling. A new coinage has even entered the lexicon — haek-gaein, "nuclear individual" — describing a degree of atomization beyond even the nuclear family. I believe this trend is deeply implicated in the rise of school bullying, depression, suicide, mental illness, inflammatory bowel disease, and chronic lifestyle diseases. Oxytocin is not merely beneficial for the body. It is a secret weapon for an entire life. What if we ate with the people we love instead of eating alone? What if we gossiped more freely, exercised together, played together, visited our parents more often? I want to show you how oxytocin consoles the troubled heart, how it makes us better and more generous, how it deepens our capacity for love and sharpens our sense of responsibility. Will you stay with me for this story? Discovering oxytocin transformed my own life — it resolved problems I had carried for years and made everything richer. My deepest hope is that this book will do the same for you: that your life will become happier, fuller, and more beautiful for having read it.

Imagine a wonder drug. Take it once, and you become kinder. You notice what is good in people rather than what is wrong. When someone stumbles, you see the world through their eyes, forgive them, and respond with grace. This same drug prevents obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer. It treats chronic pain, insomnia, anxiety, depression, and inflammatory bowel disease. And if a teenager on the autism spectrum takes it, that young person begins — for the first time — to read emotions on another person's face.

If such a drug existed, would you want to know about it?

It exists. It is oxytocin — produced in the posterior pituitary, deep within the hypothalamus. In Part 1, I am going to tell you everything about this extraordinary substance.
