Part Two: Oxytocin for the Soul

Chapter 5: The Power That Conquers Trauma

— Oxytocin

A Morning the World Split in Two

In September 2001, I was a doctoral student in Canada, walking to work at the Steadward Centre, a disability athletics facility on the University of Alberta campus. The moment I stepped through the door, I knew something was wrong. Everyone was staring at a large television mounted on the wall, their faces locked in the kind of stillness that precedes catastrophe. The air itself felt different — cold, heavy, stripped of the casual warmth of an ordinary morning.

What's going on?

I looked up at the screen. Black smoke was pouring from one of the World Trade Center towers. A building fire, I thought. Nothing more. I turned to walk toward my office. Just as my fingers touched the door handle, a woman's scream — sharp, involuntary, almost animal — tore through the room behind me.

I spun around. The crowd was gasping — "Oh — oh — oh!" — and there, on the screen, a second plane drove itself into the upper floors of the adjacent tower. Cries of "Oh my God!" erupted from every corner. I stood frozen, voiceless, as if someone had poured concrete into my shoes. We all stared. Some wept. Some prayed. And then, moments later, both buildings collapsed — crumbling like sandcastles under an indifferent tide.

Just like that, the world was divided into before and after.

How We Face Catastrophe

For days I sat in front of the television, drowning in fear and helplessness. The rescue workers' desperate efforts saved many lives, but 343 firefighters from the New York City Fire Department perished. Most of those who survived were afterward haunted by a special kind of torment: the guilt of having lived when their brothers did not. Some, unable to bear the weight of that guilt, eventually took their own lives. In addition to the firefighters, thirty-seven Port Authority police officers, twenty-three NYPD officers, eight paramedics, and one patrol officer died that day. The trauma radiated outward in concentric circles — from the responders to their families, from their families to entire communities. A 2017 American report revealed that the number of firefighters who died by suicide was more than double the number who died in the line of duty. A 2015 journal article surveyed four thousand paramedics and found that a staggering 37 percent had contemplated suicide; roughly 7 percent had actually attempted it.1

Two decades later, the wounds have not healed. A recent study tracked the mental health of 4,815 people who experienced the attacks and found that 13.44 percent still suffer from PTSD. Those carrying trauma experience more than three times the cognitive and physical impairment of those without it. Their grip strength has diminished. Their legs have weakened. Trauma is not an imaginary condition hovering in the ether of the mind — it writes itself into the body. PTSD elevates systemic inflammation and increases the risk of metabolic syndrome, atherosclerosis, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and cancer. A recent Harvard School of Public Health study of nurses found that women with PTSD symptoms and depression faced nearly four times the risk of death compared to their unaffected peers.2

Oxytocin: A New Weapon Against Trauma

Childhood emotional and physical abuse leaves scars that do not fade with time. They deepen. Trauma sustained in youth increases the risk of depression, psychiatric illness, physical disease, and premature death well into adulthood. The very word "trauma" descends from the Greek for "wound" — and wounds, as it turns out, form as readily on the soul as on the skin. Body and mind are not the two separate substances Descartes imagined; they are woven so tightly together that a gash in one bleeds into the other. A wound to the body scars the psyche, and a wound to the psyche manifests as genuine physical disease. The DSM-5 defines trauma as "an extremely frightening, dangerous, or shocking event — experienced or witnessed — that threatens health or causes impairment."3

But recently, a radical new idea has entered the conversation. What if successfully navigating an experience severe enough to cause PTSD could make you not merely whole again, but stronger than you were before?

This is the concept of Post-Traumatic Growth, or PTG. In the late 1980s, psychologist Richard Tedeschi was working with people with disabilities and their families, studying pathways to psychological relief, when he noticed something the existing literature had largely overlooked: certain individuals experienced genuine growth — even positive transformation — under conditions of extreme stress, including cancer. In the early 1990s, Tedeschi met Lawrence Calhoun, who had been independently studying disaster and loss, and together they gave this phenomenon a name and a framework. Where traditional psychology focused on resilience — the restoration of patients to their pre-trauma baseline — Tedeschi and Calhoun discovered something more radical. People could find positive meaning even amid rape, incest, cancer, heart attack, AIDS, the death of a loved one, war, and the Holocaust. Some of them grew more deeply human because of what they had suffered, not merely in spite of it.4

They classified these transformations into three categories. First, a changed sense of self: people who had endured life-altering events reported that they had grown in ways they could not have predicted. Second, transformed relationships: many expressed profound gratitude for the family and friends who had stood beside them during illness or crisis, and they saw, as if for the first time, how precious those people truly were. Even some survivors of sexual assault became more proactive in caring for themselves and more intentional in their connections with others. Third, a new philosophy of life: cancer survivors and disaster survivors began to value their lives differently. Their priorities shifted. Where wealth and professional success had once sat at the summit, they now placed time with loved ones, travel with family, the irreplaceable texture of shared experience. This landmark paper attracted explosive global attention upon publication and has since been cited more than eight thousand times.

So is post-traumatic growth related to oxytocin? People who were abused as children carry elevated lifelong risks — impaired social functioning, higher disease rates, suicidal ideation, death from cardiovascular disease or cancer. Research shows that individuals with mutations in the oxytocin receptor gene face heightened vulnerability to depression and PTSD following childhood abuse. A study of sixty-three adults with social anxiety found that among those with oxytocin receptor mutations, emotional trauma correlated with a physically smaller hippocampus — that seahorse-shaped region critical to memory and learning. But here is the crucial pivot: among those who had received strong social support, the hippocampus did not shrink. The architecture of the brain itself had been protected by the presence of other people.

These findings suggest that oxytocin is directly implicated in PTSD risk — and that even individuals with receptor mutations can substantially reduce their vulnerability if the people around them show up.

Can inhaling oxytocin actually improve PTSD symptoms? Yes. When patients with PTSD were given oxytocin to inhale over eight days, those with the most severe symptoms showed marked improvement. Another study found that even a single administration reduced PTSD symptoms and elevated feelings of well-being. Multiple lines of research have converged on the same conclusion: oxytocin genuinely alleviates PTSD.

In my own work, I have spent years studying cancer patients, particularly the ways in which exercise relieves their symptoms. I have seen breast cancer patients recover upper-limb function in a single month — a recovery that typically requires at least a year. I have watched colorectal cancer patients who had to visit the bathroom more than thirty times a day return to normal function after just six weeks of exercise. The gains in mental health and quality of life that come along the way are almost too dramatic to believe.5

Until recently, I attributed these improvements entirely to exercise. But a new thought has been forming: could some of the symptom relief and the surge in quality of life be driven, at least in part, by oxytocin? And if so, would exercising alongside other people — rather than alone — amplify the effect? It is a hypothesis worth testing.

Oxytocin in Everyday Life: Want to go for a run together? Exercise boosts oxytocin, and when you run side by side, trading encouragement, your worries and anxieties have a way of dissolving before you realize they are gone. If running isn't for you, try a neighborhood badminton circle or a local sports club.
