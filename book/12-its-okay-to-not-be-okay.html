Part Three: The Oxytocin That Makes Us Better People

Chapter 12: It's Okay to Not Be Okay

A few years ago, the Korean drama It's Okay to Not Be Okay became a phenomenon. Its tagline promised "a fairy-tale romance between Gang-tae, a psychiatric ward caretaker who refuses love under the crushing weight of life, and Moon-young, a children's book author who has never known love due to an innate flaw — as they heal each other's wounds and learn to care." In truth, how many of us could look at someone with psychopathic traits and say, with a straight face, "That's okay with me"? Public fear of psychopaths has only intensified in recent years, stoked by a drumbeat of headline-grabbing crimes.

Perhaps that is why every suspect in a high-profile violent crime in Korea now seems to undergo a psychopathy assessment. In 2022, the "Busan roundhouse kick man" — who brutally assaulted a woman he encountered on the street — and Jung Yu-jung, who murdered a woman her own age and then dismembered and disposed of the body, scored 27 and 28 points, respectively, on the psychopathy checklist. The average person scores around 15; anything above 25 indicates pronounced psychopathic tendencies. These details were dutifully reported beneath the headlines.

What Makes a Psychopath's Brain Different?

The story of James Fallon is especially riveting. A neuroscientist and professor at the University of California's medical school, Fallon was comparing the brain scans of psychopaths and serial killers when he stumbled onto a discovery that turned his world inside out: his own brain bore a striking resemblance to those of the psychopaths he had been studying. Using fMRI, he had been cataloguing the brains of various criminals and had noticed a consistent pattern — diminished activation in the regions responsible for empathy and moral reasoning. One day, while comparing these criminal scans with those of Alzheimer's patients and family members used as controls, he noticed that one family member's scan looked eerily like a psychopath's. Consumed by curiosity, he tracked down whose scan it was. It was his own. He published the story in the book The Psychopath Inside.1

Fallon argues that most psychopaths show abnormalities in the limbic system — the brain's emotional core. This is why they struggle to empathize with others' feelings. He focuses in particular on the amygdala and the anterior cingulate cortex. The amygdala processes instinctive fear and anxiety, while the orbital cortex — a region of the frontal lobe — acts as the brake that inhibits violent impulses triggered by these primal emotions. When the orbital cortex malfunctions, the result is a profoundly dangerous person. When both the amygdala and the orbital cortex are compromised, you get a psychopath. When the amygdala functions normally but the orbital cortex is impaired, you get a loose cannon — someone who cannot rein in their temper, who acts on impulse, who lurches from one reckless decision to the next.

Dr. Fallon puts it in terms that send a chill down the spine: "Psychopaths are not incapable of reading other people's minds. Sometimes they read people as though they had telepathic powers. What they cannot do is feel what the other person feels."

What does oxytocin do in the orbital cortex? A fascinating study offers a glimpse. Forty male participants inhaled either genuine oxytocin or a placebo, after which researchers stroked their skin with three different implements: a plastic brush, a coarse burlap brush, and a soft goat-hair brush. When asked to describe the sensation, participants who had inhaled oxytocin consistently reported greater pleasure — regardless of which brush was used. Brain imaging confirmed heightened activation in their orbital cortex. Could oxytocin genuinely help people with antisocial personality disorder? Meta-analyses examining the link between oxytocin inhalation and antisocial behavior do exist, but the evidence remains divided — some studies report promising effects, while others counsel caution.2

Why Empathy Matters

A rich body of research on oxytocin and empathy has produced results that are as illuminating as they are moving. One of the most compelling studies involved prairie voles — small, unassuming rodents celebrated in the scientific literature for their lifelong monogamous pair bonds. Researchers separated a female prairie vole from her mate, then subjected her to electric shocks and piercing sirens to induce stress and trauma. When the shaken female was returned to her partner's cage, the male immediately rushed to her side, pressing his body against hers, licking and grooming her with a tenderness that can only be described as devotion. But when researchers blocked oxytocin's effects by administering an antagonist, the same once-affectionate male did not so much as glance at his distressed partner. He simply went on eating his food, utterly indifferent to her presence. The doting lover had become, overnight, something resembling a psychopath. The hormone that had made this tiny creature one of nature's greatest romantics was none other than oxytocin. Published in one of the world's premier scientific journals, the study was recognized as the first to scientifically demonstrate that oxytocin powers not only empathy for others but the active impulse to comfort those who are suffering.3

Look around you. Some people are exquisitely attuned to the pain of others; some seem constitutionally incapable of feeling it. If oxytocin's absence truly prevents us from sensing another person's suffering, might it be possible to transform someone with psychopathic tendencies by restoring it? The research methodology is straightforward: measure blood oxytocin levels, check for mutations in oxytocin receptors, and assess the person's capacity for empathy. Studies have shown that individuals with oxytocin receptor mutations had markedly more difficulty empathizing with others' pain and struggled even to imagine suffering in their minds. Further research revealed that people with oxytocin receptor abnormalities engaged in more frequent and more severe marital conflicts — including wielding the threat of divorce as a weapon — and experienced more crises in their relationships than comparison groups. The more severe the receptor mutation, the higher the individual scored on the autism spectrum, with pronounced deficits in social interaction and communication.

Taken together, the evidence is clear: oxytocin is, without question, the hormone of empathy. But what about those whose oxytocin levels are naturally low — what hope is there for them? Patience. Later in this book, you will find detailed, practical methods for raising your oxytocin.4

Oxytocin Booster: When someone is speaking to you, listen with intention. Alternate your gaze gently between their eyes and mouth. Nod from time to time. This simple act builds a bridge of shared understanding between you and the speaker — and your oxytocin will rise.

The Oxytocin Reconciliation Method

No relationship sails through calm waters forever. Even people who get along beautifully can find themselves snapping at each other over the most trivial things. When oxytocin is high, we are naturally more inclined to see the situation from the other person's perspective, which makes conflict less likely to ignite. But what if the damage is already done? What if you and someone you care about have fallen out — and the fault is yours?

First, apologize unconditionally — even to the point of overdoing it. I once stood in a long Starbucks line, finally ordered, and received the wrong drink. When I brought it to the barista's attention, she apologized so profusely and bowed so deeply that I felt embarrassed — and my irritation evaporated on the spot. As she remade my drink, I overheard her murmur to the trainee beside her: "When you apologize, go overboard like that, immediately. Understood?" She had been training the new employee all along. I could not help but laugh, and I walked away with a lesson worth more than the coffee.

Second, make peace over a good meal. The other person may be too angry to break bread with you. But if you can manage it, sharing something delicious while you talk will raise their oxytocin levels and increase the odds that they will understand — and forgive.

Third, make the other person feel trusted. When someone believes you trust them, their brain responds with a surge of oxytocin. You can say it directly, or you can enlist a mutual friend, or send a letter, or choose a gift that speaks for you. Remember: forging a new relationship is important, but healing a fractured one is no less so.
