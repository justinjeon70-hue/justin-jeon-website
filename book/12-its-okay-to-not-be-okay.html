Part Three: The Oxytocin That Makes Us Better People

Chapter 12: It's Okay to Not Be Okay

A few years ago, the Korean drama It's Okay to Not Be Okay became a massive hit. Its tagline described it as "a romantic comedy fairy tale about a psychiatric ward caretaker, Gang-tae, who refuses love under the weight of a burdensome life, and a children's book author, Moon-young, who has never known love due to an innate flaw — as they heal each other's wounds." But honestly, how many people could really say, "My partner might be a psychopath, but that's okay with me"? Negative perceptions of psychopaths have only intensified in recent years as more psychopathic criminals have appeared in the news.

Perhaps that is why, it seems, every suspect in a recent high-profile violent crime in Korea undergoes a psychopathy assessment. In 2022, the "Busan roundhouse kick man," who brutally beat a woman he encountered on the street, and Jung Yu-jung, who murdered a woman her own age and dismembered the body, scored 27 and 28 points, respectively, on the psychopathy checklist. The average person scores around 15, while scores above 25 indicate strong psychopathic tendencies — details that were dutifully reported beneath the headlines.

What Makes a Psychopath's Brain Different?

The story of James Fallon is particularly fascinating in this context. Fallon, a professor at the University of California medical school and a neuroscientist, was conducting comparative research on the brains of psychopaths and serial killers when he made a startling discovery: his own brain closely resembled those of the psychopaths he was studying. Using fMRI scans, he had been comparing the brains of various criminals and noticed reduced activation in the regions responsible for empathy and moral reasoning. While comparing these scans with those of Alzheimer's patients, he stumbled upon a family member's brain scan that bore an uncanny resemblance to a psychopath's. Driven by curiosity, Fallon began investigating which family member it was — only to discover that the scan was his own. He published these findings in the book The Psychopath Inside.1

In the book, Fallon argues that most psychopaths show abnormalities in the limbic system, which governs emotions. This explains why psychopaths struggle to empathize with others' feelings or situations. He focuses particularly on the amygdala and the anterior cingulate cortex. The amygdala handles instinctive fear and anxiety responses, while the part of the frontal lobe that inhibits violent impulses triggered by these emotions is the orbital cortex. When the orbital cortex malfunctions, the result is a highly violent and dangerous person. When both the amygdala and orbital cortex are compromised, you get a psychopath. When the amygdala is normal but the orbital cortex is impaired, the person becomes impulsive and volatile — someone who cannot control their temper and is prone to erratic, reckless behavior.

Dr. Fallon puts it this way: "Psychopaths are not incapable of reading other people's minds. In fact, they sometimes read people as if they had telepathic powers. What they cannot do is feel what the other person feels." A chilling observation.

What does oxytocin do to the orbital cortex? There is a fascinating study on this. Forty male participants inhaled either real oxytocin or a placebo, after which their skin was stroked with a plastic brush, a burlap brush, and a goat-hair brush. When asked how each brush felt, participants who had inhaled oxytocin consistently reported feeling more pleasant sensations, regardless of which brush was used. Brain imaging also showed greater activation in their orbital cortex. Could oxytocin actually help people with antisocial personality disorder, the kind seen in psychopaths? Meta-analyses examining the relationship between oxytocin inhalation and antisocial personality disorder do exist, but the evidence is mixed — some studies report positive effects, while others urge caution.2

Why Empathy Matters

A rich body of research on oxytocin's relationship to empathy has produced fascinating results. One of the most compelling studies involved prairie voles — small rodents known for their monogamous pair-bonding. Researchers separated a female prairie vole from her mate and subjected her to electric shocks and blaring sirens to induce stress and trauma. When the traumatized female was returned to a cage with her male partner, the male immediately approached her, pressed his body against hers, and groomed her with devoted tenderness. But when researchers administered an oxytocin antagonist to block oxytocin's effects, the same affectionate male did not even glance at his distressed partner. Instead, he busied himself with his own food, completely ignoring her. The once-loving vole appeared to have become a psychopath overnight. As it turned out, the hormone that had made this tiny rodent one of nature's greatest romantics was none other than oxytocin. Published in one of the world's top scientific journals, this study was the first to scientifically demonstrate that oxytocin is a powerful force not only in empathizing with others but in comforting those who are suffering.3

Look around you, and you will see that some people are deeply attuned to others' pain, while others seem utterly incapable of empathy. If it is true that oxytocin's absence prevents us from feeling another person's suffering, then could oxytocin possibly transform someone with psychopathic tendencies? The research approach is straightforward: measure a person's blood oxytocin levels, check for variations in their oxytocin receptors, and assess their capacity for empathy. Studies have found that people with mutations in their oxytocin receptors had significantly more difficulty empathizing with others' pain and struggled even to imagine suffering in their minds. Other research showed that individuals with oxytocin receptor abnormalities had more frequent and severe marital conflicts — including threats of divorce — and experienced more relationship crises than comparison groups. The more severe the receptor mutation, the higher the scores on the autism spectrum assessment, with significant problems in social interaction and communication.

All of this suggests that oxytocin is, without question, the hormone of empathy. But what about people whose oxytocin levels are naturally low? Hold that thought. Later in this book, you will find detailed methods for raising your oxytocin levels.4

Oxytocin Booster: When someone is speaking to you, listen attentively while alternating your gaze between their eyes and mouth. Nod occasionally. This creates a sense of shared understanding between you and the speaker, and your oxytocin levels will rise.

The Oxytocin Reconciliation Method

No relationship can be smooth sailing all the time. Even people who get along well can end up bickering over the smallest things. When oxytocin levels are high, we are more inclined to empathize with the other person's situation and try to understand their perspective, which means arguments are less likely to erupt. But what if you have already fallen out with someone? And what if the fault is yours? What should you do?

First, apologize unconditionally — even to the point of overdoing it. I once waited in a long Starbucks line, only to receive the wrong drink. When I complained, the barista apologized so profusely and bowed so deeply that I felt embarrassed, and my anger dissolved instantly. As she remade my drink, I overheard her say to a new trainee beside her: "When you apologize, go overboard like that, right away. Got it?" She had been training the new employee the whole time. I could not help but laugh, but I walked away with a valuable lesson.

Second, make peace over a good meal. The other person may be too angry to sit down with you. But if you can manage it, sharing a delicious meal while talking will raise their oxytocin levels and increase the likelihood they will understand and forgive you.

Third, convince the other person that you trust them. When someone believes that you trust them, their oxytocin secretion is stimulated. You can communicate this trust directly, or you can enlist a third party, or use a letter or a gift to convey your feelings. Remember: building a new relationship is important, but mending a broken one matters just as much.
