Part Three: The Oxytocin That Makes Us Better People

Chapter 13: Oxytocin for Becoming a Better Parent

These days, "no kids zones" — establishments that refuse entry to children below a certain age — have become a major social issue in Korea. As a parent, it can feel a bit harsh, but on the other hand, you can empathize with the business owner's frustration. No one appreciates parents who sit idle while their child rolls around on the floor making a scene, disturbing everyone around them. I used to find the noise of children annoying. But at some point, I began to enjoy the sound of children chattering and playing with carefree abandon. When the sound of children laughing and running in the playground drifted through my window, I would feel my heart warm and my body relax, almost without realizing it.

In mice, a female that has never given birth dislikes the squeaking of other mice's pups and may even kill them. In contrast, a female with mothering experience feels boundless love for pups and will care for any infant, even another mother's offspring. In one experiment, every time a mother mouse pressed a button, researchers placed a pup in her cage. The mother pressed the button up to one hundred times per hour, displaying an extraordinary intensity of maternal devotion. The hormonal changes that occur during pregnancy and birth are what create this fierce maternal drive.

A Mother's Qualification: Oxytocin

Humans are remarkably similar. When mothers were shown photographs of their babies while undergoing fMRI scans, distinctive changes appeared in their brains — specifically in the regions that generate the deep maternal love and emotional readiness needed to care for a child.1

When a baby needs help, it simply cries. Hungry? It cries "I'm hungry." In pain? It cries "I'm hurting." Wet diaper? It cries "I'm uncomfortable." Crying is a baby's language, and mothers respond to this language instinctively. An infant's cry normally activates the mother's brain, compelling her to rush to the child. But in mothers with severe postpartum depression, this brain region does not activate in response to crying. An infant's cry stimulates the mother's anterior insula and prefrontal cortex. The insula is the brain region that allows us to feel another person's pain as our own — what scientists often call the "empathy center." It integrates signals from throughout the body and acts as a conductor, coordinating our emotions and regulating the frontal lobe. In cases of severe depression or drug addiction, a mother may hear her baby crying and feel nothing. Mothers whose prefrontal cortex does not respond to their baby's cries are at significantly higher risk of child abuse. Given the disturbing frequency of child abandonment cases in the news today, one can reasonably suspect that these mothers had extremely low oxytocin levels.2

The Secret to Parenting: Oxytocin

Do parents who abuse or abandon their children come from dysfunctional homes where they themselves received no love? If so, they too are victims. Indeed, infant mice that were not cared for by their mothers have low oxytocin levels as adults and, when they have their own pups, neglect or even kill them. Monkeys raised by humans in zoos also show low oxytocin levels. What about children who were abandoned by their parents and raised in orphanages? Research shows that children raised in institutional care have larger amygdalae compared to control groups and exhibit far more anxious temperaments.* They carry more internalized problems, and when shown photographs of frightening faces, their amygdalae activate intensely. The neural connectivity between their amygdalae and frontal lobes is also diminished, which means that even when they experience negative emotions, their ability to regulate those emotions through the frontal lobe is impaired.3

Parents with higher oxytocin levels respond immediately to their baby's cries and make significantly more eye contact with their children. Parents who inhale oxytocin interact more smoothly with their children and become more proactive about protecting their child from unfamiliar people who might pose a threat. The oxytocin circulating in a parent's bloodstream flows into the brain, amplifying affection and attentiveness toward the child.

The evidence, taken as a whole, paints a clear picture: a mother's oxytocin during pregnancy influences her child's social development; couples in more intimate relationships have higher oxytocin; parents' oxytocin levels are deeply linked to their children's oxytocin levels; and these effects extend into adulthood, shaping how those children eventually raise their own offspring. When you consider all of this together, the conclusion is inescapable: children raised by happy parents grow up to be happier, more socially adept, and more resilient.4

\* The above research was not conducted in Korea. Additionally, not all individuals raised in institutional care have enlarged amygdalae.

When we talk with someone, maintaining steady eye contact is a remarkably important social skill. Imagine explaining something to someone, only to see them staring at their phone or gazing elsewhere — even if they happen to be looking up something related to your topic, you would feel slighted. You would probably think, "They are not listening to me at all." Oxytocin helps us look into each other's eyes and develop more positive feelings toward one another. And those who gaze into each other's eyes produce even more oxytocin in return — a true virtuous cycle of oxytocin and eye contact. Next, let us explore the effects of oxytocin on love.
