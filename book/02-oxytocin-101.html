Chapter 2: Oxytocin 101 -- The Hormone That Has Protected Humanity

-- The Hormone That Has Guarded Humanity

The Discovery of Oxytocin

Oxytocin was first identified in 1906 by the British pharmacologist Sir Henry Hallett Dale. To appreciate how remarkable that is, consider that dopamine — a hormone most of us now take for granted — was not discovered until the 1950s. In an era of primitive instruments and fragmentary knowledge, Dale intuited that chemical messengers must exist within the brain, ferrying signals between nerve cells. He called them neurotransmitters. Working with pregnant cats, he extracted a substance from the posterior pituitary gland and showed that it caused the uterus to contract. He named it oxytocin, from the Greek for "swift birth." He then confirmed his findings by injecting the substance into the uteri of laboratory rats. For his pioneering work on oxytocin and his later discovery of other neurotransmitters — acetylcholine and histamine among them — Dale was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1936.1

Nearly four decades passed before the next great leap. American biochemist Vincent du Vigneaud determined the molecular structure of oxytocin and then accomplished what had seemed impossible: he synthesized it in the laboratory. The molecule turned out to be a peptide hormone* composed of just nine amino acids and a sulfur atom. For comparison, insulin — another peptide hormone — contains fifty-one amino acids. Oxytocin's compact size is the reason it can be delivered today as a simple nasal spray. Du Vigneaud received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry the following year for his achievement.1

[Hormones fall into two broad categories: peptide hormones and steroid hormones. Peptide hormones act through receptors on the cell surface; steroid hormones pass through the membrane and act directly on the cell nucleus. For more detail, see the "Hormone 101" chapter.]

How does oxytocin orchestrate the drama of childbirth? As delivery approaches, the posterior pituitary — nestled beneath the hypothalamus — begins releasing oxytocin in rising quantities. The hormone travels through the bloodstream, locks onto receptors, and commands the uterus to contract. Each contraction triggers a fresh wave of oxytocin, which drives an even stronger contraction. Through this elegant feed-forward mechanism, the intervals between contractions shorten and their force intensifies until the mother's body expels the child. At the peak of labor, a woman's oxytocin levels may reach ten to forty times their baseline.2

That same torrent of oxytocin serves as a natural analgesic, blunting the searing pain of delivery. It promotes the healing of the uterine lining and the birth canal. It ignites the maternal instinct with an almost irresistible force, compelling the mother to cradle her newborn and nurse. In animal experiments, mothers deprived of oxytocin neglected their young after birth. In the most extreme cases, they killed them.

Oxytocin is essential to breastfeeding. The more vigorously a baby suckles, the more oxytocin the mother's body produces. As oxytocin rises, milk flows more freely, and the baby nurses with greater intensity. Mother and child thus raise each other's oxytocin in a virtuous spiral from which both benefit. Extraordinary, is it not? Through this mechanism, a new mother recovers with remarkable speed. Oxytocin is central to both a successful delivery and the nourishment that follows.2

The hormone also plays a vital role in an infant's brain development. When oxytocin is released in a nursing baby's brain, it increases blood flow to the neocortex and cerebral cortex, supporting overall neural growth. Its impact on the neocortex is particularly significant: this region constitutes 80 percent of brain volume and is responsible for abstract reasoning, inference, and the logical thinking that underpins language and mathematics. The implication is profound — a mother's physical touch is not merely comforting to a newborn; it is architecturally essential for the developing brain. Whether breastfed or bottle-fed, when an infant makes eye contact and has skin-to-skin contact with a parent during feeding, oxytocin levels climb even higher. Animal studies have shown that pups separated from their mothers for as little as three hours a day grew into adults with diminished sociability and compromised immune systems.3

Most of us know that breast milk strengthens an infant's immunity. It contains a beneficial bacterium called Lactobacillus reuteri. But recent research revealed a troubling disparity: when scientists tested breast milk for Lactobacillus, roughly half of rural mothers carried it — while urban mothers had almost none. Given the well-documented collapse of gut microbial diversity in city dwellers, it seems likely that not long ago, breast milk universally contained abundant Lactobacillus, regardless of geography. Recent studies suggest that this bacterium may help improve symptoms of autism and stimulate the body's own oxytocin production. Paired with evidence that oxytocin accelerates wound healing, breastfeeding emerges as critical not only for the mother's recovery but for the infant's brain development and lifelong social functioning.4

Oxytocin: Commander of the Low-Birth-Rate Task Force

In truth, oxytocin begins its work long before delivery. From the moment two people meet, fall deeply in love, and feel the magnetic pull of attraction, this hormone is already on the job, shaping desire and conception alike. Consider the humble roundworm Caenorhabditis elegans — it resembles an earthworm but is one hundred to one thousand times smaller. Because of its genetic kinship with humans, it is a workhorse of modern laboratory science. When researchers disabled the oxytocin gene or the oxytocin receptor gene in these tiny creatures, the males could barely find females. Those that did struggled to locate the female's reproductive opening. And even those that managed that much failed to deposit their sperm. The result: reproduction ground nearly to a halt.5

What about humans? Before two people can become intimate, they must first recognize each other — and recognition must kindle attraction. Does oxytocin play a role in both? The short answer is: unequivocally, yes. Even in animals, when oxytocin fails to function, something resembling collective face-blindness sets in. The animals cannot recognize one another and cannot mate. Rodents rely on scent rather than sight to identify their companions, so when oxytocin is impaired, identifying a potential partner takes dramatically longer. Humans, of course, recognize and remember one another primarily by face.

Research has demonstrated that oxytocin sharpens facial recognition. Multiple studies show that inhaling oxytocin improves not only the ability to identify faces but also the ability to remember someone after a single meeting. In a 2013 study, researchers scanned the brains of eighteen adult men with fMRI while showing them photographs of various women. In one session the men inhaled oxytocin; in another, a placebo. Under the influence of real oxytocin, their attraction to the women in the photographs increased — not because dopamine surged, but because negative emotions receded. We will explore the connections between oxytocin, trust, and altruism in much greater depth later.6
