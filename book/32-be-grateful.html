Chapter 32: Be Grateful, and It Goes Up -- The Secret of Gratitude

, and It Goes Up — The Secret of Gratitude

One of my favorite sayings about human relationships is this: "Gratitude begets greater gratitude." When I receive a gift, I believe its true value lies not in the price tag but in the thank-you card that comes with it. Even if the gift is not expensive, a heartfelt, handwritten card transforms it into the most priceless treasure in the world, one that moves me deeply.

On the other hand, even the most luxurious designer gift, if it arrives without a card, feels like a generic off-the-shelf product. It may be useful, but it does not inspire.

Last year, while setting my annual goals, I thought of the many people who had helped me along the way. I recalled each face and wrote a long email — not a handwritten letter, I admit — to each of them, specifically describing how they had helped me and what had come of that help. As I wrote, a broad smile spread across my face, and in some cases, tears fell. It was a profoundly happy and meaningful exercise for me, and the replies I received radiated happiness in return.

Gratitude Rewires the Brain

I am raising two sons. Well, I am not really raising them — they are growing up remarkably well on their own, and for that I am grateful. My older son is currently in Canada, looking after his younger brother, a high school senior eight years his junior. The fact that both boys left the nest allowed my wife — who had spent her entire life supporting the children and me, never once putting her own career to use — to finally start working in Korea. I cannot express how grateful I am.

When the boys were still living with us, we had a tradition of taking turns leading a prayer of gratitude on Sundays. These were not selfish prayers asking for personal wishes but expressions of thanks for one another, for the circumstances we had been given, for our family, and for every blessing we could identify. Something remarkable happened: during weeks when we practiced gratitude prayers, our second son's behavior was noticeably more positive and cooperative. Even his adolescent rebellion seemed to calm down. At the time, I wondered skeptically, "Could gratitude prayers really be taming a teenager's rebellious streak?"

Then I encountered the research of Professor Antonio Damasio at the University of Southern California, and I was stunned. His findings empirically confirmed what I had only vaguely sensed. Damasio and his team hypothesized that when we feel grateful, the brain regions governing morality and value judgments would be activated. To test this, they showed participants interview footage of Holocaust survivors who had narrowly escaped death in Nazi concentration camps thanks to the help of others. While the participants watched, fMRI scans monitored their brain activity.

The results showed that participants' degree of gratitude was strongly correlated with activation in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). The medial prefrontal cortex is a critically important brain region that governs planning, execution, impulse control, meaning-making, and concentration. The anterior cingulate cortex regulates emotions, perceives pain, and plays a profound role in reward expectation, decision-making, impulse control, and morality. Could the transformation I had noticed in my son during those gratitude-prayer weeks have been caused by these very brain changes?1

Gratitude Summons Oxytocin

Earlier I mentioned that when I receive a gift, I focus not on the gift itself but on the thank-you card that accompanies it. There is a study from the University of Tsukuba in Japan that speaks directly to this. Researchers had one coworker write a letter of gratitude to another and read it aloud. They then examined the brain activity of the person hearing the letter. Not only was the prefrontal cortex activated, but feelings of anger, depression, fatigue, and anxiety decreased, and the listener shifted into a more positive emotional state. A single heartfelt letter can transform a person's life this dramatically. The premise of calling humans "social animals" lies in reciprocity: we are creatures who express emotions to one another and respond to those expressions. Do not deny it or resist it. That is simply how we are built. We respond to expression.2

Recently, I decided to splurge and enrolled my second son in a summer camp. After he returned safely, I called him, secretly hoping he would express some appreciation for the considerable expense. He responded with the exact opposite: "Why did you sign me up for such a terrible camp?" Complaints and grievances poured out. I felt the rage rising and was about to fire back, but with superhuman restraint, I held my tongue. "No — I need to steer this toward a conversation of gratitude, of positivity." I took a deep breath and said, "But wasn't it nice living with kids you'd never met before? Wasn't it fun?"

Slowly, my son began finding the positive aspects of his experience. When one person expresses genuine gratitude, it is only human nature that the recipient feels warmed and wants to do even more to deserve that gratitude.

Research supports this too: when someone hears an expression of gratitude, they become kinder not only to the person who expressed it but to unrelated third parties as well. Gratitude begets greater gratitude, and that gratitude ripples outward, ultimately making the world a more livable place. This is reminiscent of the idea in the film Pay It Forward, in which 11-year-old Trevor, given the school assignment to "find a way to make the world a better place," devises a network of gratitude and reciprocity. He performs an act of kindness for three people and tells each of them: "Don't thank me — pay it forward to someone else."3

What makes this even more fascinating is that the medial prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex are rich in oxytocin receptors. This could mean that people with higher oxytocin levels express gratitude more frequently. It could also mean that the exchange of grateful expressions raises oxytocin. Professor Paul Zak's research team had 41 adults over the age of 65 inhale oxytocin for 10 days and then administered a battery of psycho-physiological tests. The most significant change? A greater sense of gratitude.

We already know that mutations in the CD38 gene — which is involved in oxytocin release — or in the oxytocin receptor variant rs3796863 are associated with reduced sociality and higher likelihood of autistic traits. Recently, researchers trained couples in gratitude exercises and then measured how much time they voluntarily spent together. The training was simple: "If your partner does something you appreciate, take the time to say 'thank you.'" The result? Couples spent more time together across virtually every domain of life and felt more positive emotions toward each other. The study scientifically proved that CD38 was involved in mediating these effects. The fascinating connection? CD38 is also closely linked to immune function. It is possible that every time we cultivate a grateful heart, our immune systems strengthen and our bodies grow healthier. So instead of just popping health supplements to boost immunity, try telling your family and friends that you are thankful first.4

The Oxytocin Gratitude Method Gratitude is one of the most important emotions in life. Gratitude begets greater gratitude. There is no one among us who has nothing to be grateful for. No one is more unfortunate than the person who never feels grateful. The more you practice gratitude, the more things worth being grateful for seem to appear. Gratitude is a lifestyle, a habit. Some people are always searching for reasons to complain, while others are always searching for reasons to give thanks. Grateful people are surrounded by those who care for them; complaining people are surrounded by no one. Gratitude raises oxytocin and activates the prefrontal cortex in both the giver and the receiver. When gratitude activates the prefrontal cortex, you begin to assess your situation more objectively and develop in a direction that solves problems constructively. How can you become a more grateful person? Start a "gratitude club" with people you see regularly. Just meet up, chat, share things you are grateful for, and make a habit of cheering each other on. You can start with a spouse, coworker, or friend. Two is fine, but aim for at least three. Meet regularly — whether once a month, once a week, or every day — rain or shine, with or without a specific occasion. The best way to become a grateful person is to regularly write thank-you letters to the people who matter most to you. Handwritten letters are best. If that feels too hard, email works. Even a text message is fine. And if you can attach a small gift — it does not have to be expensive; even a coffee gift card will do — the impact of your gratitude will more than double. So let us start right now. Everyone, pick up your phones.
