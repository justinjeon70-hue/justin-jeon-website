Chapter 3: Hormone 101 -- The Vast Chemical Factory Called the Human Body

-- The Vast Chemical Factory Called the Human Body

The word hormone derives from a Greek verb meaning "to set in motion," and that is exactly what hormones do. Produced by the endocrine glands and released into the bloodstream, they course through the body orchestrating metabolism and development, growth and maintenance, reproduction and response to change. More than eighty have been identified so far — familiar names like estrogen, testosterone, dopamine, endorphins, adrenaline, and insulin among them. The emotions that sweep through us without warning? Hormones at work. The physical differences between men and women? Hormones again. Each is manufactured in a different part of the body, serves a distinct purpose, and operates through its own mechanism. The human body, in this sense, is a chemical factory of staggering complexity.

Hormones circulate in the blood and touch virtually everything — emotion, appetite, body weight, energy metabolism, and diseases from diabetes to cardiovascular disease to cancer. There is scarcely an aspect of bodily function they do not influence. This is why pregnancy can feel like an emotional earthquake. Two hormones drive the upheaval: estrogen and progesterone. Estrogen is the architect of well-being — it elevates mood and sharpens cognition. Progesterone can do the opposite, triggering irritability and anxiety. During pregnancy, these two hormones fluctuate wildly, sending emotions on a roller coaster. The same hormonal seesaw, playing out across the menstrual cycle, is why mood variability tends to be more pronounced in women than in men.

Are women the only ones at the mercy of hormonal tides? Far from it. During andropause — the male equivalent of menopause — testosterone plummets while estrogen actually rises. This is why a man who embodied stoic masculinity in his youth may, in middle age, develop a surprising enthusiasm for doing the dishes, find himself weeping openly at television dramas, or grow wistful at the sight of falling leaves. If you notice a man of a certain age who has suddenly become chatty or begun seeking out sentimental ballads from decades past, the wisest course is to leave him in peace. Just think to yourself, "Ah — the estrogen is kicking in," and let him be.

Every hormone discussed so far belongs to the category of sex hormones. What of the rest? Insulin, cortisol, and growth hormone are among the best known. These hormones are secreted into the bloodstream and circulate through the entire body — brain, cardiovascular system, organs, and muscles alike. They are indispensable for brain function, cardiovascular performance, digestion, immune defense, and muscular movement. Without them, the body cannot operate.

Hormones are divided into two broad families based on their molecular architecture. Peptide hormones are built from proteins and act through receptors stationed on the cell surface. It does not matter how much peptide hormone the body produces — if the receptors are blocked or dysfunctional, the signal never gets through, and problems arise immediately. Oxytocin, the protagonist of this book, is a peptide hormone. Steroid hormones, by contrast, are constructed from cholesterol and other lipids. They need no surface receptor; they pass directly through the cell membrane and act on the nucleus. The most prominent steroid hormone is cortisol.

Cortisol is released from the adrenal cortex — a small gland perched atop each kidney — when the hypothalamic-pituitary axis secretes adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) in response to stress or low blood sugar. It raises blood sugar, dampens immune function, and ramps up the metabolism of fat and protein. When chronic stress keeps cortisol perpetually elevated, it becomes a driving force behind diabetes, abdominal obesity, muscle loss, and immune suppression. Yet cortisol is not purely destructive. In the short term, it triggers acute inflammatory responses that protect the body from harm — some wound-healing ointments are, in fact, designed to mimic its effects. The danger lies in the chronic state: when cortisol stays elevated day after day, it erodes immunity and opens the door to metabolic disease, depression, and a constellation of other mental health conditions.

These hormones influence the entire body — brain, organs, and everything in between — which is why they are inseparable from our emotions, our immune defenses, our muscle mass, our body composition, and the diseases that threaten us. But here is the crucial insight: our daily habits also shape hormone secretion and function. We are not passengers on a hormonal ride with no steering wheel. Through regular exercise, sound nutrition, positive thinking, and activities that dissolve stress, we can bend our hormones to our will — making them work for us, rather than ruling over us.
