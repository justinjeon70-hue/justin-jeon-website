Chapter 3: Hormone 101 -- The Vast Chemical Factory Called the Human Body

-- The Vast Chemical Factory Called the Human Body

The word hormone generally refers to all chemical substances produced by endocrine organs. Derived from a Greek word meaning "to set in motion," hormones flow through the bloodstream, governing our body's metabolism, development, growth and maintenance, reproduction, and responses to change. There are more than 80 known hormones, including familiar ones like estrogen, testosterone, dopamine, endorphins, adrenaline, and insulin. Our constantly shifting emotions? That's the handiwork of hormones. The physical differences between men and women? Also shaped by hormones. Each hormone is produced in a different part of the body, serves a different function, and operates through a different mechanism. In short, the human body is a vast chemical factory.

Hormones circulate in our blood and influence our emotions, appetite, body weight, and energy metabolism, as well as diseases ranging from diabetes and cardiovascular disease to cancer. There is virtually no aspect of basic physical function or disease that hormones don't touch. So why do pregnant women experience such dramatic mood swings? It comes down to two of the body's primary female hormones: estrogen and progesterone. Estrogen is the "feel-good" hormone — it promotes happiness and even improves cognitive function. Progesterone, on the other hand, can make you irritable and anxious. During pregnancy, the dramatic fluctuation of these two hormones leads to intense emotional ups and downs. This is also why mood swings are more commonly observed in women than in men — because of the cyclical rise and fall of estrogen and progesterone throughout the menstrual cycle.

Do only women experience hormone-driven emotional changes? Not at all. During male menopause (andropause), testosterone drops sharply while estrogen actually increases. This is why a man who was the picture of rugged masculinity in his youth might, in middle age, develop a fondness for doing the dishes, find himself weeping at TV dramas, or grow sentimental at the sight of falling autumn leaves. If you know a man in this phase of life who has suddenly become chatty or started seeking out old songs from his youth, the best advice is: don't poke the bear. Just think, "Ah, the estrogen is kicking in," and let him be.

All of the hormones mentioned so far are sex hormones. What about the others? Insulin, cortisol, and growth hormone are among the most well-known non-sex hormones. These hormones are secreted into the bloodstream and circulate throughout the entire body — reaching the brain, cardiovascular system, organs, and muscles. They are essential for brain function, cardiovascular performance, digestion, immune function, and muscle movement. Without them, the body simply cannot operate.

Hormones are classified into two major categories based on their molecular composition: peptide hormones, made of proteins, and steroid hormones, made from cholesterol and other lipids. Peptide hormones work through receptors on the cell surface. No matter how much of a peptide hormone the body produces, if the receptors are blocked or dysfunctional, problems arise immediately. Oxytocin, the subject of this book, is a peptide hormone. Steroid hormones, by contrast, do not require surface receptors — they pass directly into the cell and act on the nucleus. The most prominent steroid hormone is cortisol.

Cortisol is released from the adrenal cortex (located atop the kidneys) when the hypothalamic-pituitary system secretes adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) in response to stress or low blood sugar. Cortisol raises blood sugar, suppresses immune function, and increases fat and protein metabolism. When chronic stress keeps cortisol perpetually elevated, it becomes a driving force behind diabetes, abdominal obesity, muscle loss, and immune suppression. However, cortisol also plays a protective role: it triggers acute inflammatory responses to shield the body from harm. Some wound-healing ointments are, in fact, designed to mimic cortisol's effects. The danger lies in chronically elevated cortisol, which can suppress immunity and contribute to metabolic disease, depression, and various other mental health conditions.

These hormones influence the entire body — brain and organs included — which is why they are intimately connected to our emotions, immune function, muscle mass, body fat, obesity, and a wide range of diseases. But here is the crucial point: our lifestyle habits also influence hormone secretion and function. Rather than surrendering our bodies to hormonal whims, we must actively take charge of our hormones. Through regular exercise, healthy eating, positive thinking, and stress-relieving activities, we can make our hormones work for us — not the other way around.
