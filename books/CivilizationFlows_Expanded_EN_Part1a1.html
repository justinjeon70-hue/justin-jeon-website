Civilization Flows

By Prof. Justin Jeon

Prologue: The Black Bird and the White Bird

In 1938, the Dutch artist M.C. Escher completed a woodcut titled Day and Night. Two flocks of birds fly across the composition in opposite directions: black birds soar leftward over white Dutch farmland bathed in daylight, while white birds glide rightward over the same landscape darkened into night. The genius of the work lies not in the birds themselves but in the space between them. Each bird's outline is simultaneously the negative space that defines the bird flying beside it in the opposite direction. The white birds do not merely accompany the black birds; they constitute them. Remove the white flock and the black flock does not simply lose a companion — it ceases to exist. The two are not parallel; they are reciprocal. They are one image perceived as two, one reality rendered in complementary tones. This single woodcut, hung in galleries from Amsterdam to Tokyo, holds within its tessellated geometry a truth that most histories of civilization have refused to confront: that East and West are not separable narratives but interlocking forms, each giving shape to the other, each meaningless in isolation.

I first encountered this problem not in a gallery but in a high-school classroom in Seoul, at the age of seventeen. The year's curriculum was divided, as it had always been divided, into two parallel tracks: Korean History and World History. Korean History began with Gojoseon and moved through the Three Kingdoms, Goryeo, and Joseon in a self-contained arc. World History began with Mesopotamia and Egypt, passed through Greece and Rome, and arrived at the European Enlightenment as though following a torch relay in which no East Asian hand ever carried the flame. The two courses shared a building but never a sentence. I raised my hand and asked the question that would take me forty years to answer: "Why do Korean history and world history never meet?" The teacher paused, offered a kind smile, and said, "They are different subjects." That answer was not wrong in any bureaucratic sense. But it was, I would come to understand, a symptom of a far deeper fracture — a fracture not in the curriculum but in civilization's self-image.

The fracture followed me across continents. In British Columbia, I sat inside a longhouse with a First Nations elder who described the shaman's journey through three cosmic layers — an upper world of spirits, a middle world of the living, a lower world of the dead — while drums pounded in steady, hypnotic rhythm. I had heard those drums before. I had seen that three-layered cosmos before. They belonged to the Korean gut ritual, performed by mudang shamans whose ecstatic trances, layered cosmology, and percussive invocations I had witnessed since childhood. The resonance was too precise to be coincidence, too structural to be mere analogy. In the British Museum, I stood before a case of Hallstatt-period pottery from Iron Age Central Europe, its surface decorated with horizontal bands of zigzag lines, chevron hatching, and dot-stamped rows. My breath stopped. The decorative grammar was virtually identical to the comb-pattern pottery (bitsalmunui togi) excavated at Amsa-dong on the banks of the Han River in Seoul — pottery dated to approximately 8000 BCE, some six thousand years older than its European echoes. In Gyeongju, the ancient Silla capital, I examined gold crowns whose tree-and-antler motifs, dangling gold leaf ornaments, and granulation techniques bore startling resemblance to the gold work of the Scythian nomads buried across the Eurasian steppe from the Black Sea to the Altai Mountains. And at Bangudae, on a riverside cliff in Ulsan, I traced with my eyes the outlines of whales carved into rock seven or eight thousand years ago — carvings whose hunting iconography, weapon systems, and ecological knowledge of cetacean migration would reappear, millennia later, among the Inuit whalers of Alaska's North Slope.

Each encounter deepened the same suspicion: the line that separates East from West is not a natural boundary but a drawn one, and it was drawn with a particular hand. That hand belonged, in its most philosophically influential form, to Immanuel Kant. In his 1764 essay Observations on the Feeling of the Beautiful and Sublime, Kant arranged human cultures along a developmental axis in which European civilization occupied the position of maturity — humanity's "adulthood" — while the cultures of Asia were consigned to an earlier stage, a "childlike" phase of aesthetic and moral development (Kant, 1764, Ak. II: 243-256). This was not a passing remark but a structural claim: it positioned Europe as the telos of history and everyone else as its preamble. Hegel would later systematize this into a full philosophy of history in which the "Oriental World" served merely as the unconscious dawn before the Greco-Roman day (Hegel, 1837/1956, The Philosophy of History). The consequences were enormous. The developmental line — from primitive to advanced, from East to West, from child to adult — became the invisible architecture of modern historiography, the reason my high-school teacher could say "They are different subjects" without sensing any violence in the separation.

This book is a rebuttal of that line. But I must be precise about what kind of rebuttal it offers. It does not claim that Eastern civilization is superior to Western civilization. To reverse the line — to place Seoul where Kant placed Berlin, to substitute one hierarchy for another — would be to accept the line's validity while merely flipping its direction. A reversed line is still a line. What I propose instead is the abolition of the line itself and its replacement with a different geometry: the cycle. Not a line but a cycle. Not hierarchy but resonance. Not superiority but flow. The movement of civilization, as the evidence assembled in these chapters will show, is not a one-directional march from a single origin to a single destination. It is a circulation — a perpetual exchange of technologies, symbols, rituals, and ideas across the Eurasian landmass, the Pacific Rim, and the maritime corridors that connect them, flowing eastward and westward, northward and southward, in patterns that no linear model can capture.

The deepest symbol I know for this pattern is also one of the oldest: the Yin-Yang (陰陽). Two forces, opposite and complementary, each containing the seed of the other, each flowing into the other in an endless rotation that generates all change. The black bird and the white bird. Day and night. East and West. Not two stories but one story told in two voices, each voice audible only because the other is singing. This is the structure of civilization as I have come to understand it after four decades of walking its roads — twelve roads, to be exact, each tracing a distinct current of cultural flow across continents and centuries. The Whale Road from Bangudae to Alaska. The Comb-Pattern Road from Korea to Finland and the Celts. The Jade Road from Hongshan to Mesoamerica. The Gold Crown Road from the Scythian steppe to Silla. The Iron Road, the Silk Road, the Spice Road, the Shamanic Road, the Music Road, the Ondol Road, the Alphabet Road, and the Philosophy Road. Each road carries its own cargo of evidence — archaeological, genetic, linguistic, mythological — and each challenges, in its own way, the Kantian line that still governs how we teach and think about human history.

Escher's birds are still flying. The question is whether we have the courage to see them as he drew them: not two flocks but one image, not two civilizations but one flow.

Chapter 1: The Whale Road — From Bangudae to Alaska

A Cliff Face at the Edge of Time

On the banks of the Daegok River in Ulsan, in the southeastern corner of the Korean peninsula, a vertical rock face rises roughly three meters high and stretches ten meters wide. Its surface is covered with approximately three hundred figures carved into the stone: whales, dolphins, sea turtles, deer, tigers, humans wielding harpoons, boats filled with hunters, and scenes of collective pursuit at sea. This is the Bangudae Petroglyphs (Bangudae Amgakhwa), designated National Treasure No. 285 by the Republic of Korea, and it constitutes one of the most extraordinary archaeological documents on Earth — not because of what it depicts, but because of when it was made and what it implies about the people who made it.

The carvings have been dated, through analysis of the patina layers, associated sediment stratigraphy, and stylistic comparison, to approximately 7,000-8,000 years before the present. To grasp the magnitude of this date, consider the following: the Great Pyramid of Giza was constructed around 2560 BCE, roughly 4,500 years ago. The earliest known writing systems — Sumerian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphs — emerged around 3200 BCE. Bangudae predates the pyramids by at least four thousand years and the invention of writing by at least three thousand. When the unknown artists of Bangudae pressed their stone tools into that riverside cliff, most of the civilizations that populate "World History" textbooks did not yet exist. And yet what these artists recorded was not primitive. It was, by any functional definition, a portrait of civilization.

The World's Oldest Whaling Scenes

In 2004, the French anthropological journal L'Anthropologie published a landmark study by Lee Sang-mok and Robineau in which the Bangudae carvings were identified as the world's oldest confirmed depictions of organized whale hunting (Lee & Robineau, 2004, L'Anthropologie 108(1): 137-151). The claim rested not on a single image but on the systematic character of the scenes: multiple boats, coordinated positioning, harpoons attached to lines attached to floats, and the depiction of different whale species in different behavioral postures — breaching, diving, spouting, and nursing calves. This was not the illustration of an occasional, opportunistic beaching. It was the record of a sustained, technologically sophisticated, and ecologically informed hunting tradition.

Consider what organized whale hunting requires. First, it requires seaworthy vessels — boats capable of operating in open coastal waters where large cetaceans feed and migrate. The Bangudae carvings depict multi-person boats with raised prows, suggesting craft built for stability in ocean swells. Second, it requires a composite weapon system: a harpoon with a detachable head, a line of sufficient length and tensile strength, and an inflated float (typically a sealed animal bladder or skin) to create drag on the wounded animal and prevent it from sounding to an unreachable depth. All three components — harpoon, line, and float — appear in the Bangudae panels. Third, it requires collective tactics: the coordination of multiple boats to encircle, drive, and exhaust a whale before the final kill. The petroglyphs show precisely this, with boats arranged in crescent formations around fleeing whales. Fourth, and perhaps most remarkably, it requires deep ecological knowledge. The whale species most prominently depicted at Bangudae is identifiable, by its body proportions and the absence of a dorsal fin, as the gray whale (Eschrichtius robustus). The gray whale is a coastal migrator that enters shallow waters to breed and calve — exactly the conditions in which it is most vulnerable to hunters in small boats. The Bangudae artists knew which species to target, where it would appear, and when.

The Gray Whale's Road

The gray whale does not stay in Ulsan. Its annual migration traces one of the longest mammalian journeys on the planet. The western Pacific population — the population that would have been hunted at Bangudae — feeds in the waters off Sakhalin Island and the Sea of Okhotsk during summer, then migrates southward along the coasts of the Korean peninsula and Japan to breed in warmer waters during winter. The eastern Pacific population follows a parallel route along the North American coastline, feeding in the Bering and Chukchi Seas during summer and migrating south to the lagoons of Baja California for winter breeding. The total round-trip migration distance ranges from 16,000 to 20,000 kilometers, making it one of the longest known migrations of any mammal (Kato & Kasuya, 2002, Journal of Cetacean Research and Management 4(1): 7-15).

This migratory path is not merely a biological fact. It is a cultural corridor. The gray whale's route connects the coastal waters of Korea to the Sea of Japan, northward to Sakhalin, across to Kamchatka, through the Bering Sea, and down to the Alaskan and Pacific Northwest coasts. Every community along this corridor that hunted the gray whale faced the same technical challenges and, the evidence suggests, arrived at strikingly similar solutions. The whale's road became humanity's road — a maritime highway along which not only harpoons but ideas, rituals, and social structures traveled.

Five Parallel Evidence Lines

The connection between the whaling cultures of prehistoric Korea and those of the North Pacific and Arctic is supported by at least five independent lines of evidence, no single one of which would be sufficient but whose convergence creates a cumulative case that is difficult to dismiss.

First: the harpoon-line-float weapon system. The composite toggling harpoon — a harpoon head designed to detach from the shaft upon impact and rotate beneath the whale's skin, remaining anchored while the shaft falls away — appears in both the Bangudae carvings and the archaeological record of Aleut and Inuit whaling communities across the North Pacific. Savelle and McCartney, in their extensive survey of prehistoric whale hunting technology, documented this weapon system across Thule and earlier Arctic cultures, noting its functional sophistication and its remarkable consistency across vast distances (Savelle & McCartney, 1999, World Archaeology 30(3): 375-394). The system's core architecture — toggling head, braided line, inflatable float — is identical in principle from Ulsan to Barrow.

Second: heating technology. Archaeological excavations at Aleutian Island sites have uncovered under-floor heating channels remarkably similar to the Korean ondol system, in which heated air or smoke from a fire pit is directed through stone channels beneath a living surface to provide radiant floor heating. While convergent invention is always possible, the specificity of the design — the channeled sub-floor architecture rather than simple central hearths — invites serious comparative analysis.

Third: whale masks and ritual objects. Both Korean coastal communities and Pacific Northwest/Alaskan indigenous peoples developed elaborate ritual practices surrounding the whale hunt, including carved masks representing whale spirits, ceremonial preparations involving the shaman's mediation between the human community and the animal world, and post-hunt rituals of gratitude designed to ensure the whale's spirit would return in future seasons.

Fourth: shamanism. The shamanic traditions of Korea (musok) and those of Siberian, Aleut, and Inuit peoples share a structural core that goes well beyond superficial resemblance: the shaman's ecstatic trance induced by rhythmic drumming, the journey of the shaman's soul through a three-layered cosmos (upper, middle, and lower worlds), the use of animal spirit helpers, and the shaman's role as mediator between the human community and the forces of nature. Eliade's classic comparative study identified this circumpolar shamanic complex as one of humanity's oldest and most widespread religious structures (Eliade, 1964, Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy, Princeton University Press).

Fifth: totem poles and jangseung. The carved wooden poles of the Pacific Northwest — totem poles depicting stacked animal and spirit figures — find a structural parallel in the Korean jangseung, carved wooden or stone poles placed at village entrances as guardian figures. Both traditions use vertical carved poles as markers of community identity, spiritual protection, and cosmological orientation. The parallel extends to Siberian serge posts and other vertical ritual markers found across the North Pacific cultural zone.

The Coastal Migration and the Kelp Highway

How could such connections exist across such distances? The answer lies in a paradigm shift that has transformed our understanding of how the Americas were first populated. For most of the twentieth century, the dominant model held that humans entered the Americas via the Bering Land Bridge (Beringia) and then moved southward through an ice-free corridor between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets approximately 13,000 years ago. This model placed the first Americans firmly in the role of terrestrial big-game hunters following mammoths across a treeless land bridge.

The coastal migration hypothesis, now supported by a growing body of evidence, proposes an alternative: that the first Americans traveled not across the interior of Beringia but along its southern coast, following a "kelp highway" — a continuous belt of productive kelp forest ecosystems that stretched from Japan and Kamchatka along the Aleutian Islands and down the Pacific coast of North America. Erlandson and Rick articulated this model in detail, arguing that maritime-adapted peoples with boat technology and the ability to exploit kelp forest resources — fish, shellfish, sea mammals, seabirds — could have moved rapidly along this coastal corridor at a date significantly earlier than the ice-free corridor would have permitted (Erlandson & Rick, 2010, Journal of Island and Coastal Archaeology 5(2): 265-280). The kelp highway did not require ice-free interior passages. It required boats and the knowledge of the sea — precisely the capabilities documented at Bangudae.

In 2021, the paradigm shifted further. Fossilized human footprints discovered at White Sands, New Mexico, were dated to approximately 23,000 years before the present — a full ten thousand years earlier than the Clovis-first model had allowed (Bennett et al., 2021, Science 373(6562): 1528-1531). At 23,000 BP, the interior ice-free corridor was sealed shut by glaciers. The only plausible entry route was coastal. The people who left those footprints were, in all probability, the descendants of maritime-adapted populations who had followed the Pacific Rim southward — populations whose cultural ancestors may well have hunted whales off the coast of what is now Korea.

"Bangudae is not a drawing. It is evidence of civilization — a civilization that mastered the sea, organized collective enterprise on a scale that required social complexity, and generated ecological knowledge that would not be surpassed for millennia. The artists of Bangudae did not merely depict whales. They documented a way of life that would flow, along the whale's own migratory road, across the entire breadth of the Pacific."

Chapter 2: The Comb-Pattern Road — Korea to Finland and the Celts

A Grammar Written in Clay

In 1925, archaeological excavations at Amsa-dong, on the north bank of the Han River in what is now eastern Seoul, unearthed fragments of pottery unlike anything previously described in the standard typologies of world ceramics. The vessels were large, with pointed or rounded bottoms, and their surfaces were covered with intricate patterns made by pressing a comb-like tool — a multi-toothed implement, perhaps a shell edge or a carved bone — into the wet clay before firing. The patterns were not random. They were organized into horizontal bands that encircled the vessel from rim to base: a band of short diagonal slashes, then a band of zigzag lines, then a band of herringbone or chevron hatching, then a band of dot-stamped rows, and so on. This is bitsalmunui togi — comb-pattern pottery — and it has been dated, at its earliest horizons, to approximately 8000 BCE, making it one of the oldest ceramic traditions in East Asia and, indeed, in the world (Choe & Bale, 2002, Journal of World Prehistory 16(2): 143-197).

Four thousand years later and five thousand kilometers to the northwest, potters in what is now Finland began producing vessels with a strikingly similar decorative program. Finnish Comb Ceramic Ware (kampakeraaminen kulttuuri), dated to approximately 4200 BCE, displays the same organizational principle: horizontal bands of comb-stamped decoration arranged in zones around the vessel, featuring zigzag lines, hatched bands, rows of impressed dots, and chevron patterns. The similarity was noted by Finnish archaeologists as early as the mid-twentieth century, but it was long treated as coincidence or convergence — after all, a comb is a simple tool, and zigzags are a simple motif. The resemblance, it was assumed, meant nothing.

Move another three thousand years forward and two thousand kilometers to the southwest, to the salt-mining settlement of Hallstatt in the Austrian Alps, the type site of the early Celtic Iron Age (c. 800 BCE). Here, too, pottery surfaces were decorated with banded arrangements of geometric motifs — zigzags, chevrons, hatched zones, dot rows — organized in horizontal registers around the vessel body. When I stood before a case of Hallstatt ceramics in the British Museum, the decorative grammar was so close to what I had seen at Amsa-dong that the temporal and spatial gulf between them seemed, for a vertiginous moment, to collapse.

Grammar, Not Vocabulary

The key insight is not that individual motifs recur. A zigzag line is, as skeptics correctly observe, among the simplest marks a human hand can make. A row of dots requires nothing more than a pointed stick. If the parallel consisted only of isolated motifs — a zigzag here, a dot row there — it would prove nothing. What makes the Amsa-dong / Finland / Hallstatt parallel significant is not the vocabulary but the grammar: the system by which motifs are organized. In all three traditions, decoration is arranged in horizontal bands that zone the vessel surface from rim to base. Within each band, a single motif is repeated continuously. Bands alternate in a rhythmic sequence. The overall effect is a structured, layered composition in which the vessel's surface becomes a register of patterned information, not a canvas for freeform drawing. This decorative grammar — horizontal-band zoning with systematically alternating geometric motifs — is not a universal feature of early pottery. Many ceramic traditions worldwide use scattered, all-over, or vertically oriented decoration. The specific grammar shared by these three traditions is a cultural choice, not a geometric inevitability.

And it moves from east to west. Korea, approximately 8000 BCE. The Baikal-Amur region and the Russian Far East, 6000-5000 BCE. The Ural-Volga region, 5000-4000 BCE. Finland and the eastern Baltic, 4200-3000 BCE. Central Europe, 1000-500 BCE. The chronological gradient is consistent and unidirectional. The grammar appears first in East Asia and spreads westward across the Eurasian forest-steppe belt, arriving in northern Europe roughly four to six millennia after its East Asian origins. This is not diffusion of a single pot or a single motif; it is the westward propagation of a decorative system — a way of organizing visual information on a three-dimensional surface that remained recognizably coherent across thousands of years and thousands of kilometers.

Where the Pot Goes, the People Go

For decades, the dominant paradigm in archaeology resisted the idea that pottery styles could serve as tracers of population movement. Pots, it was argued, are ideas, and ideas travel without people. A community can adopt a neighboring group's ceramic style through trade, imitation, or cultural prestige without any genetic exchange. This was the processual orthodoxy, and it made diffusionist arguments about pottery deeply unfashionable.

Then came the ancient DNA revolution. In 2018, a study published in Nature analyzed the genomes of 400 individuals associated with the Bell Beaker cultural complex — a pottery style that spread across Western Europe between approximately 2750 and 2200 BCE. The results were seismic: in Britain, the arrival of Bell Beaker pottery was accompanied by a genetic replacement of approximately 90 percent of the existing population (Olalde et al., 2018, Nature 555: 190-196). The pots did not travel alone. The people traveled with them. The Bell Beaker study did not prove that every pottery style in history marks a migration, but it shattered the assumption that pottery never marks migration. It reopened the possibility that when a decorative grammar moves across a continent, it may be carried by the hands that made it.

This finding reframes the comb-pattern parallel. If the decorative grammar that appears in Korea at 8000 BCE and in Finland at 4200 BCE was carried by people rather than transmitted as a disembodied idea, then we are looking at a deep-time population movement along the Eurasian forest-steppe belt — a movement of communities who carried their ceramic identity with them as they expanded westward over millennia. The pot is not a coincidence. The pot is a passport.

Function Adapts, Grammar Endures

One of the most telling features of the comb-pattern parallel is the way in which functional form changes while decorative grammar remains stable. Korean comb-pattern pottery characteristically has a pointed or conical bottom. This is a functional adaptation to the sandy, soft substrates of Korean Neolithic living surfaces and coastal shell middens: a pointed vessel can be pushed into sand or supported in a bed of ash and will stand upright without a flat base. Finnish Comb Ceramic Ware, by contrast, typically has a flat or slightly rounded bottom — an adaptation to the hard-packed earth and wooden floors of subarctic dwellings where a pointed vessel would topple. The functional engineering of the pot changed in response to local environmental conditions. But the decorative grammar — the system of banded zones, the repertoire of zigzag, chevron, hatching, and dot-row motifs — persisted. Function follows ecology; grammar follows identity. The potters of Finland were solving different engineering problems than the potters of Amsa-dong, but they were speaking the same decorative language. This pattern — adaptive divergence in function combined with conservative retention of symbolic grammar — is precisely what we would expect from a migrating population adjusting to new environments while maintaining its cultural identity.

Beyond the Pot: Language, Heat, Bears, and Spirits

The ceramic parallel does not stand alone. It is embedded in a wider network of structural similarities between Korean and Uralic (Finnish-Estonian-Hungarian) cultures that collectively suggest deep historical connection rather than shallow coincidence.

Language. Korean and Finnish are both agglutinative languages — languages that build complex words by stringing together discrete morphemes, each carrying a specific grammatical function, rather than relying on inflection or word-order change. Both exhibit vowel harmony, a phonological system in which the vowels within a word must belong to the same harmonic class (front or back, bright or dark). Both follow a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) word order. Neither possesses grammatical gender. These typological parallels have long been noted by linguists, though the precise genealogical relationship between Korean and the Uralic family remains debated. In 2021, Robbeets and colleagues published a major study in Nature proposing that the "Transeurasian" languages — a grouping that includes Turkic, Mongolic, Tungusic, Koreanic, and Japonic — share a common origin linked to the spread of millet agriculture from the West Liao River region of northeastern China approximately 9,000 years ago (Robbeets et al., 2021, Nature 599: 616-621). If this model is correct, the linguistic parallels between Korean and Finnish may reflect not direct genealogy but shared deep roots in a broader Eurasian agricultural expansion — the same westward flow that carried the comb-pattern grammar.

Heating culture. The Korean jjimjilbang (public bathhouse and heated-floor resting space) and the Finnish sauna are often compared at a superficial level as "bathing cultures." The parallel runs deeper. Both traditions center on the social and spiritual practice of communal heating — the use of intense, enclosed heat not merely for hygiene but for relaxation, social bonding, spiritual purification, and health. Both cultures treat the heated space as semi-sacred. Both integrate the practice into daily life to a degree unmatched by most other cultures. The Korean ondol (under-floor radiant heating) and the Finnish habit of building saunas before houses share a common assumption: that managed heat is not a luxury but a civilizational foundation.

Bear worship. Both Korean and Finnish mythological traditions accord the bear a unique sacred status. The Korean foundation myth of Dangun involves a bear (gom) that endures a trial of darkness and deprivation to become human and give birth to the nation's founder. Finnish and Sami traditions include elaborate bear funeral rituals (peijainen) in which the slain bear is treated as a honored guest, its skull mounted on a sacred tree, and its spirit ritually returned to the forest to ensure future abundance. The bear is not merely an animal in either tradition; it is an ancestor, a mediator between the human and spirit worlds, a figure of transformation. Bear worship of this structural type — involving ritual killing, ceremonial treatment of remains, and belief in the bear's spiritual return — is attested across the entire circumpolar zone, from Korea through Siberia to Scandinavia, forming what scholars have called the "circumpolar bear ceremonialism" complex (Hallowell, 1926, American Anthropologist 28(1): 1-175).

Shamanism. As noted in the Prologue, the shamanic traditions of Korea and northern Eurasia share a structural core: ecstatic trance, drumming, three-layered cosmology, animal spirit helpers, and the shaman as community mediator. In the Finnish tradition, the tietäjä (seer or knower) performed functions closely parallel to the Korean mudang — healing, divination, communication with spirits, and maintenance of the community's relationship with the forces of nature. The shamanic parallel is not a separate phenomenon from the ceramic parallel; it is part of the same cultural package.

Ex Oriente Lux

In 2009, the archaeologists Peter Jordan and Marek Zvelebil published a landmark volume titled Ceramics Before Farming: The Dispersal of Pottery Among Prehistoric Eurasian Hunter-Gatherers, in which they advanced the thesis of Ex Oriente Lux — "light from the East" — arguing that the invention and spread of ceramic technology across northern Eurasia originated in East Asia and moved westward, carried by hunter-gatherer communities along the forest-steppe belt (Jordan & Zvelebil, 2009, Routledge). Kuzmin's extensive radiocarbon survey of early pottery sites across Eurasia had already established the chronological gradient: the oldest pottery in the world appears in East Asia (China, Japan, the Russian Far East, and Korea) between 20,000 and 10,000 years ago, and the technology spreads progressively westward, reaching the Baltic and Scandinavia by approximately 5000-4000 BCE (Kuzmin, 2006, Journal of World Prehistory 20: 1-42). The comb-pattern road is a specific instance of this broader east-to-west flow: a decorative grammar born in Korea, carried westward through the taiga and steppe, and deposited in Finnish and eventually Central European ceramics over the course of six to eight millennia.

The implications are profound. The standard narrative of technological civilization — in which innovation radiates outward from the "Fertile Crescent" or, later, from Europe — does not account for the ceramic evidence. Pottery, one of humanity's foundational technologies, originated in the East and flowed West. The comb-pattern grammar, one of humanity's earliest systematic decorative programs, originated in Korea and spread across the continent. The light, in this case, came not from Mesopotamia or Athens but from the banks of the Han River. This does not make Korea the "center" of civilization in place of Europe — to make such a claim would be to fall back into the very linear, hierarchical thinking this book opposes. What it demonstrates, rather, is that the flow of civilization is multidirectional, that innovations arise in many places and travel in many directions, and that the decorative grammar pressed into wet clay by a Neolithic potter at Amsa-dong eight thousand years before Christ was robust enough, meaningful enough, and portable enough to survive a journey of ten thousand kilometers and six thousand years. That is not coincidence. That is civilization in motion.

"The pot is not a coincidence. The pot is a passport — a document of identity carried across continents by hands that remembered, through millennia of migration, how their ancestors had marked the clay."
