Wound, Relationship, and Maturity
Chapter Twelve

The Wounded Healer

A Theology of the Boundary

In the spring of 2015, commuting by bicycle in Boston, I took a bad fall. I had come down a hill and was rounding a corner, slipping past a stopped car.

I threw the bike and flung my body; my left hip struck the rail tracks, my body rolled, and I landed on the tracks face first. A broken front tooth felt with the tongue, blood mixed with the spit I gave out. My head protected by the helmet, my shoulder laid open, my front tooth driven in. Someone calling an ambulance, someone bringing tissues, a child looking on from an opened car door, my bicycle lying where it fell.— 2015, Facebook

That day, with my face in ruins, I wrote something down. I wrote that my mind had grown clearer — no, that my soul had grown clearer. And then this sentence came.

Falling is a moment. That is my true form. The me who can fall is the real one. You cannot judge a person who has fallen. You cannot condemn a person who has fallen. You can only comfort, encourage, and help them up…— 2015, Facebook

Perhaps because they were written where blood was flowing, I have never been able to retract those words. Only the person who has fallen can sit down beside the fallen.

Years later, at chapel at Yonsei University, I confessed old secrets before several thousand students. A grade point average of 2.63; a heart that pounded whenever I had to present; my looks and my low self-esteem. As a child there was even a period when I had to use a separate meal tray because I was a hepatitis carrier. These were stories I had long wanted to hide. And yet that day, when I brought those wounds out, something strange happened. The auditorium went quiet, and tears welled in the eyes of some students. It was not my achievements but my wounds that reached them.

Why is it the open wound and not the hidden one that saves a person? Not long ago, letting my mother go, I learned one strand of that answer in my body. From chief mourner at her funeral to father of the groom at my son’s wedding, I had to be stout-hearted without so much as room to grieve. And there was a price for that suppression.

Denying and suppressing the sad feeling, it seemed I could feel no feeling at all. I came to understand what it means when they say a traumatized person cannot feel the signals coming from the body.— 2025, Facebook

The psychiatrist Bessel van der Kolk writes in The Body Keeps the Score that a wound one cannot bear is inscribed not in the mind but in the body. Shut down the feeling, and the body suffers that shutting. Hiding a wound is not safety; it is a way of making the body ill. If so, a wound is not something to hide but something that must somehow be opened.

Gabor Maté names this severance at a deeper level. The words that stayed with me longest from our conversation were these: the essence of trauma is not the event that happened to me but the severance from myself that the event produces. To close off feeling is to be cut off from my body and from myself. So the person who hides a wound becomes a stranger not only to others but to himself. That is why healing is the opening of the wound. Opening is being reconnected with oneself.

I went through this in my body for a long time. In the summer of 1989, my freshman year, a boat exploded during water-ski training and I suffered burns over thirty percent of my body and spent a month in hospital. It was a crossroads between life and death. But what came after the body healed lasted longer. Afraid that others would see the scars, I could not wear shorts for three years. Wearing long trousers through every summer, I learned that hiding a wound outlasts the wound.

The idea of the wounded healer is no modern invention. Sophocles’s Philoctetes, staged in 409 BCE, is already that story. On the way to Troy, Philoctetes is bitten by a snake and receives a wound that will not heal. Unable to bear the stench of the wound and the screams he gives out, the Greek army abandons him on the island of Lemnos and sails on. Ten years later an oracle declares that Troy cannot be taken without the bow of Heracles, which he possesses. The ones who abandoned him come back to fetch him.

Two things in this play have held me a long time. The first is that the wound and the bow are attached to the same man. Without the very person they threw out for his stench, they cannot win. What the community could not bear and pushed away, and what the community finally requires, are one body. The second is when the wound heals. On the island it did not heal in ten years. The oracle says he will be healed only when he reaches Troy — that is, when he has gone back among people. What would not close while he was alone closes once he returns to the company.

And the one who actually learns in this play is not Philoctetes. Neoptolemus, son of Achilles, is sent to fetch him with orders to take the bow by deceit. But watching his convulsions at close range, he is shamed and gives the bow back. It is not the man who suffered ten years but the young man who came to deceive him who is changed. This discrepancy becomes the problem again later in this chapter.

The Dutch priest and spiritual writer Henri Nouwen caught hold of this with the phrase “the wounded healer.” A healer is not someone who hides his wound but someone who makes that wound a source of healing. It is not the perfect person who mends others; it is the wounded person who reaches another’s pain through his own wound. I think of my colleague Professor Ji-Sun Lee. Burned severely over her whole body in a drunk-driving accident during her university years and having endured dozens of operations, she now teaches students and hands countless people the strength to live. She tells her own story this way: she met the accident, and then successfully parted from it. That we first met in 2003 and have remained meaningful to each other for more than twenty years is probably because we both passed through the same place. Her wound did not close; it stayed open and became a passage through which others come and go.

But I met this idea somewhere else before I learned it from Nouwen. In the summer of 2013, having finished a term of teaching and Bible study, I set down these sentences about what love is. Love is making oneself able to be wounded. The English word vulnerable means easily broken, easily wounded. If so, the measure of love changes too.

The measure of love is how much I can be wounded for that person.

The list I wrote down then was unexpectedly small. Saying “I love you” first. Saying it before hanging up the phone. Confessing one’s own weakness first. Embracing first. Giving first. Everything was first. Here is why love is a dangerous business. The hand held out first can always be refused, and the one who holds out first is always the one hurt more.

Around that same time I was watching Brené Brown’s talk on the power of vulnerability and felt my breath catch — at the passage saying that a whole person is not a perfect person. I copied it out this way: a whole person is one who accepts his own weakness and insufficiency and, by willingly opening it to view, builds up another.

Here the weight of the phrase “wounded healer” changes. It is not a technique for putting one’s wounds to use. It is standing oneself in a place where one can be wounded, and the other name for that act is love. My writing that day ended with the grain of wheat — that unless it falls into the earth and dies it remains alone, but if it dies it bears much fruit. In Chapter Eleven that verse was a story about stopping. Here it becomes a story about love. Can I fall into the ground for that person? That was the question I put to myself that day.

A person without wounds is a closed fortress. He reaches no one.

Why should that be? Because a wound is the place where my surface has been pierced. I, who was closed in a smooth shell, am opened at that place. It hurts. But only through that opened place can another come in, and only through it can I go out to another. A wound is a boundary — the place where the I inside and the world outside meet. Do you remember the hormesis of Chapter Six? A wound just short of killing made the body strong. Here the principle rises to the level of relationship. A wound just short of killing makes a person a healer. If in Chapter Six the end of “learning through suffering” was discernment about oneself, here that discernment crosses over to another. Only someone who has passed the same place recognizes where another person now stands. The Age of Coddling teaches us to hide our wounds and pretend we are fine, but a hidden wound never becomes a passage.

Still, a wound does not become a passage by itself. The behavioral economist Daniel Kahneman said there are two selves within us: the experiencing self, who lives the moment, and the remembering self, who attaches a story to that experience. That a man who found military service hard enough to want to die talks of nothing but the army decades after discharge is because he found meaning in that suffering. Suffering that cannot be named remains only as scar tissue; suffering that receives a name becomes a passage — as Moses in Chapter Nine gave two names to the same forty years.

But there is something dishonest here. I am saying that a wound becomes a passage, yet not all wounds do. The person who failed mostly just breaks, and the person in pain mostly just hurts. Suffering that cannot name itself is far more common. This was the question left over from Chapter Six. Agamemnon suffered and learned nothing. Then to whom does that learning go?

Aeschylus answered across the whole trilogy. Agamemnon returns ten years later and is killed by his wife, and the wife is killed by her son. And yet there is no traitor in this story. Agamemnon launched the expedition for his brother; the wife killed her husband for her daughter; the son killed his mother for his father. Each had someone to protect, and each was faithful to that duty. What collides is not good and evil but good and good.

That is why this cycle cannot be broken from inside. Everyone is justified. You cannot stop on the ground of justification, and bringing in a more righteous person only puts one more claimant of justification into the circuit. Here is why the trilogy ends not in reconciliation or forgiveness but in a court established in Athens: making a place to weigh right and wrong outside the circuit. So “we learn by suffering” was never, from the start, a statement about one person’s growth. It was a statement about how a community learns.

The learning did not come to those inside the cycle. Neither Agamemnon nor his wife nor his son gained anything. The wisdom came to those who watched the cycle from outside — to the audience sitting in the theater of Athens in the spring of 458 BCE.

The price is paid on the inside; the learning is harvested on the outside.

It sounds cruel, but it is the more honest account. The promise that your failure will make you grow is often a lie. But there is another sentence that can be verified: suffering that is spoken reaches someone; failure that becomes a story teaches those who did not undergo it. There is no exaggeration in that. It is what actually happened when I brought out the story of my grades and my meal tray at chapel. I recovered nothing in that moment, but some who listened wept.

And Aeschylus did not stop there. At the end of the trilogy the son never comes to understanding on his own. He does not repent, nor does he reinterpret what he did. And yet he is acquitted. The verdict is delivered by the one who lost nothing inside that circuit, the one who alone stood outside. And she does not stop at a verdict; she transforms the Furies into the Kindly Ones. The one who learned from outside changed the very nature of the circuit.

So the direction completes itself this way. The price is paid on the inside; the learning is harvested on the outside; and that learning returns inside and rescues the one who paid. If my story passed through me and reached someone, that person may one day see on my behalf what I never managed to see, and come back with it. I can be rescued even without knowing the meaning of my own failure.

Here I think of Primo Levi. He came back alive from Auschwitz and gave his life to testimony, and in his last book he wrote: we survivors are not the true witnesses; the complete witnesses are those who went all the way to the bottom, and they cannot speak because they did not return; we who survived only speak in their place. Here is why “the price is paid inside and the learning harvested outside” can never be a contented sentence. Those who suffered most deeply transmitted nothing. Every testimony we hear belongs to someone who suffered less.

Only, forget two things and this turns at once into hypocrisy. First, the one who returns cannot give back what was lost. Iphigenia stays dead to the end. Breaking the circuit and repaying the price are different things. The moment we mix them, we slide into a ledger that justifies suffering after the fact. Second, the one who learned outside is one who learned without paying. If he takes the seat of savior he most easily becomes a benefactor, and then the cycle does not break but merely changes shape into a hierarchy. The ground on which he earns the right to return is not that he is wiser but that he is in debt.

For a long time I was ashamed of my wounds. I tried to cover them with more achievement. That was the way of swelling. But what saves a person was not the covered wound but the opened one. When I stood before cancer patients, what let me reach their fear was not that I was perfect but that I too had once broken. A healer is a person who stands at a boundary — who does not close the boundary of his own wound but leaves it open, so that another’s pain may come and go through it. And between two people opened that way, what is born? That is the next chapter’s story.
