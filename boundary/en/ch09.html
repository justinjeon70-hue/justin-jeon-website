Chapter Nine

What Is Born of the Outbreath

息: The Character for Breath

One weary night, I simply let out a single long breath. It was no special meditation technique. And yet, after that one exhalation, a strange calm came over me. There is, in fact, a science to this. When you breathe out slowly, the vagus nerve we met in the last chapter switches on, the heart slows, and the body receives the signal that "it is safe now." We breathe tens of thousands of times a day, yet we are almost never conscious of the breath itself. Today I want to look, together, at a single character that concerns that breath. It is 息, the character for rest.

息 is a character formed by joining 自 (self) and 心 (heart). But 自 was originally a pictograph of the nose. And so the first meaning of 息 is breath. Beneath the nose, the heart rising and falling. Breath is a truly extraordinary thing. Among all the acts a human performs, it is the only one that is completed in every moment and yet needs no completion. Earlier we said that this is an age robbed of completion. When one project ends, the next; once one metric is filled, the next. Nothing ever ends. But breath is different. Each single exhalation is a repetition of "this is enough."

Here a deeper meaning of 息 lies hidden: offspring (子息), increase, coming-into-being. It is the 息 of interest (利息). Principal breathing, as it were, and multiplying of itself. When it comes to a person, it becomes a child (子息). But why should breath (息) and child (息) be the same character? It is no accident.

Increase comes not from clutching but from giving away.

To bear a child is to give away what is one's own. A parent spends down their own body and time, and the next life is born. A seed bursts itself open to put forth a sprout. Even interest will not multiply if you clutch the principal. It multiplies only when lent out, only when sent forth. This is what 息 says: the increase of life follows the principle not of the inbreath but of the outbreath. This is a perfect refutation of a life that only swells. Contrary to the belief that clutching makes us larger, true growth comes from the outbreath, from emptying, from spending down.

Seen this way, the outbreath is a kind of small death. With every exhalation we release the air we had just held. And, strangely, only when that small death occurs does the next breath come in. Scripture paints the beginning of the human being like this: God formed man of the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and man became a living being (Genesis 2:7). In Hebrew that breath is neshamah—breath and spirit at once. Just as the character 息 places the heart (心) beneath the nose (自), breath rises and falls at the place where body and spirit meet. And that breath was, from the very start, not my own but something breathed in—that is, borrowed. All our lives we breathe that borrowed breath in and out, until one day, with a final exhalation, we return it whole. It is precisely why our language calls death "the gathering in of breath." Life, in the end, may be nothing but breathing the borrowed breath well and returning it well.

息 holds two faces in a single body. One is cessation: to breathe one's last, a flame going out (熄). The other is bearing: offspring, procreation (生息). Within one character, stopping and life stand together. This is profound. To stop is precisely to give birth. Just as the inbreath comes only after the outbreath, the beginning comes only after the ending. And at the place where these two faces meet in fullness, the next character awaits us. It is 安息.
