Chapter Eleven

Born Where We Stop

休 · 安 · 息, and the Completion of Creation

My father, who has dementia, grows weaker and weaker. He can hardly walk now without a walker, and he has forgotten his grandson’s name — a man who once knew the whole of the Analects by heart. On the way to his home I wrote this.

Someone says: we too are fathers and mothers, but we too are beings who need a mother and a father. And yet those fathers and mothers grow weaker, and it grows harder… What frail beings we are.— 2024, Facebook

Around the same time I also wrote something on a day I had gone to the bathhouse with my father. Set the two passages side by side and you can see how one person’s place shifts.

As a small boy I went to the bathhouse holding my father’s hand. My father, who could sit a long time in the scalding sauna, was superhuman. He would sit me down and scrub my back, and when the bath was over he bought me black-bean noodles.

I go to the bathhouse holding my father’s hand. I sit down my father, who cannot get out of the tub without help, and scrape the calluses from his feet. When the bath is over I buy him black-bean noodles and sweet-and-sour pork. Bathhouse day is a glad day and a sad day.— 2023, Facebook

The same bathhouse, the same noodles, the same two people. The only thing changed is who sits whom down. Writing that, I thought about what rest a person truly needs. It was not a stretch of time doing nothing. It was a place to lean on someone and set the heart down. However strong an adult may be, he needs arms to lean into; and even the person who spent a lifetime being leaned upon someday looks for somewhere to lean. Our ancestors carved that place into three characters.

First, 休, hyu, to rest. 休 joins a person (亻) and a tree (木): a person leaning against a tree at rest. Here is the first answer. Rest is not accomplished alone. The person inside 休 is not curled up by himself; he is entrusting himself to something not himself — a tree. The tree does not demand results. It simply stays there. That very presence, lost to the person driven by achievement, is the tree in 休.

And if we step back a little, this tree may be more than a single tree. From very ancient times humanity imagined one tree standing at the center of the world — roots deep in the earth, branches touching the sky, joining heaven and earth. The Norse called it Yggdrasil; the gold crowns of Silla wore that tree upon the head; and at the center of Eden too stood the tree of life. This tree is called the cosmic tree, the axis mundi. What is interesting is that the place where this cosmic tree stands is always a boundary — the boundary of earth and heaven, of this world and the next. The place where a person truly rests is beneath that tree at the boundary.

Next is 安, an, at ease. It joins the roof radical (宀) and woman (女): a person settled beneath a roof. The ease of 安 does not come from achievement. It comes from being where one ought to be. So it is only natural that a person swelling endlessly outward is not at ease. His diagnosis is precisely 不安, an-xiety — the absence of 安. Now the two characters meet: 安息. 安 is dwelling in one’s own place, the stability of space. 息 is exhaling, ceasing, and being born anew from that emptiness — the rhythm of time. 安息, rest, is to dwell in one’s place (安) and set the breath down (息).

Where we stop, there at last we are born.

Here an old story of creation comes to mind. In Scripture God makes six days and on the seventh rests — 安息. The Hebrew shabbat means to cease, to stop. Is it not astonishing? That the last act of creation is not making more but ceasing. Creation is completed only because there is a Sabbath. Not labor but stopping ties off creation. We regard rest as an interruption of creation; Genesis says the opposite. Rest is the completion of creation. Even God declared this is enough by resting; and the person who cannot stop, refusing that 息, never arrives at enough.

And 安息 has one deeper layer still. In East Asia 安息 also means death. Rest in peace. Since 息 carries the sense of gathering up one’s breath, 安息 borders on setting the breath down entirely — that is, on death. But this death is not annihilation. As we saw in the last chapter, 息 holds ceasing and begetting in one body. So does the death of 安息. It is an end and at the same time the place of a new beginning. Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit (John 12:24). A seed must be buried (安, settled in place) and cease itself (息) before it sends up a shoot. 安息 is exactly the place of that seed.

So learning how to rest well is, in truth, also learning how to die well. Falling asleep each night is a small rehearsal of death, and every out-breath is a smaller rehearsal still. What the person who cannot stop truly fears is not rest but death — the fear that if he stops he will vanish. But only the one who knows how to stop can live fully. The old wisdom that whoever remembers death lives today is already contained in these two characters, 安息. Learning to rest well is learning to die well, and learning to die well is learning to live well.

So the next question is natural. On whom, exactly, can we lean and rest? Where is that tree? And how does it happen — as on the day I sat my father down at the bathhouse — that the one who leaned becomes the one leaned upon? That is the story of Part Four.
