Chapter Ten

Born at the Place of Stopping

休 · 安 · 息, and the Completion of Creation

My father, who suffers from dementia, grows steadily weaker. Now he can barely walk without a walker, and he has forgotten his grandson's name. This is a man who once knew the whole of the Analects by heart. On the way to his home, I wrote these words.

Someone says: though we too are fathers and mothers, we are also beings who need a mother and father. And yet that father and mother grow steadily weaker, steadily more frail… truly, we are fragile beings.— 2024, Facebook

As I wrote those words, I thought about what rest a person truly needs. It was not a time of doing nothing. It was a place of leaning on someone and setting the heart down. However strong the grown-up, one needs a bosom to lean on. That place our ancestors inscribed into three characters.

First, look at 休 (rest). 休 is a character joining a person (亻) and a tree (木). It is the figure of a person resting against a tree. Here is the first answer: rest is not accomplished alone. The person within 休 is not curled up in solitude but is entrusting himself to something that is not himself—the tree. The tree does not demand that you produce results. It simply is there for you. That very being which the person chased by achievement has lost is the tree of 休.

But if you look at this tree from a little farther off, it may be more than a single tree. From very ancient times humanity imagined a single tree standing at the center of the world. A tree that sinks its roots deep into the earth and reaches its branches to the sky, joining heaven and earth. The Norse called it Yggdrasil; the gold crown of Silla was a figure bearing that tree upon the head; and at the center of Eden, too, stood the tree of life. This tree is called the world-tree (宇宙木). What is fascinating is that the place where this world-tree stands is always a boundary. The boundary of earth and sky, the boundary of this world and the next. The place where a person truly rests is right there, beneath that tree of the boundary.

Next is 安 (ease). A character joining the roof radical (宀, a roof) and 女 (woman)—the figure of a person settled beneath a roof. The ease of 安 does not come from attainment. It comes from being in the place where one belongs. So it is only natural that the person who swells endlessly outward is not at ease. His diagnosis is, precisely, an-xiety (不安)—that is, the absence of 安. Now the two characters meet: 安息. 安 is dwelling and settling in one's proper place, the stability of space. 息 is breathing out and ceasing, and being born anew from that emptiness, the rhythm of time. 安息 is to dwell in one's proper place (安) and set the breath down (息).

At the place of stopping, one is finally born.

Here the old story of creation comes to mind. In Scripture, God makes six days and on the seventh 安息s—rests. The Hebrew shabbat means "to cease, to stop." Is it not astonishing? That the last act of creation is not making more, but ceasing. Only when there is a Sabbath is creation completed. It is not labor but the stopping that knots creation shut. We regard rest as an interruption of creation, but Genesis says the very opposite: rest is the completion of creation. Even God, by 息-ing, declared "this is enough"; and the person who cannot stop, because he refuses that 息, never arrives forever at "enough."

And 安息 has one deeper layer still. In East Asia, 安息 also means death. "May you rest (安息) in peace." Since 息 carries the sense of gathering in one's breath, 安息 borders on the setting down of breath entirely—that is, on death. But this death is not extinction. As we saw in the last chapter, 息 holds cessation and bearing in a single body. So too the death of 安息. It is an ending and, at the same time, the place of a new beginning. Unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit (John 12:24). The seed must be buried in the earth (安, settling into place) and cease itself (息) before it can at last put forth a sprout. 安息 is precisely the place of that seed.

And so to learn how to rest well is, in truth, also to learn how to die well. To fall asleep each night is a rehearsal of a small death, and each moment's outbreath is a smaller rehearsal still. What the person who cannot stop truly fears is not rest but death—that fear of seeming to vanish the moment one stops. But only the person who knows how to stop can live in fullness. The old wisdom that the one who remembers death lives today is already contained within these two characters, 安息. To rest well is to die well, and to die well is to live well.

And so the next question follows naturally. On whom, after all, can we lean and rest? Where is that tree? That is the story of Part Four.
